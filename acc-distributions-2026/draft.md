# The ACC paid its schools within $3.6 million of each other for four years. Then it started paying them for winning.

*Strip out what the ACC paid for winning and it is an equal-share conference again. Almost to the dollar.*

---

*Construction note, before any finding. Two sources carry this piece. The first is the ACC's Form 990 for the fiscal year ending June 30, 2025, retrieved through ProPublica the same way the Big Ten and SEC returns were. Per-school figures are the supported-organizations table on Schedule A, Part I, the line that cost me an afternoon in the SEC piece. The second is WRAL's August 17, 2025 reconstruction of the ACC's first-year success-initiative payments, built from a Virginia Tech Board of Visitors presentation and remarks by NC State's chief financial officer, Beverly Armwood, at a February 6, 2025 trustees meeting. That schedule isn't a table the ACC published, and I'll say so again when it matters.*

*Fiscal years don't line up across conferences. The ACC closes June 30 and the SEC closes August 31, so a row labeled 2024-25 covers a different window in each. The ACC's window still contains the whole twelve-team playoff, which ended in January 2025, so timing explains nothing here.*

*One more thing, because this publication has been running two kinds of number side by side for a week. This is filing-based work: dollars a conference reported paying to a school. The roster budgets in the last two pieces were estimates built from ranges. Don't blur them.*

## Four flat years

The whole reason the fifth year matters is what the four before it look like.

Top-to-bottom spread among ACC full-share schools:

| Season | Schools | Top | Bottom | Spread |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2020-21 | 14 | $38,133,715 | $35,318,448 | $2,815,267 |
| 2021-22 | 14 | $41,282,420 | $37,961,705 | $3,320,715 |
| 2022-23 | 14 | $46,850,044 | $43,294,354 | $3,555,690 |
| 2023-24 | 14 | $46,415,388 | $43,072,249 | $3,343,139 |
| 2024-25 | 14 | $55,129,046 | $42,814,168 | $12,314,878 |

Four years inside a band of $2,815,267 to $3,555,690. The school at the top changed every year, from Clemson to Pittsburgh to North Carolina to Louisville, and the spread barely moved, which is what an equal-shares conference looks like once it's reduced to a filing. Every row counts the fourteen football members only. The 2020-21 filing carries a fifteenth full-share school, Notre Dame, which played that COVID season as a full football member, shared its NBC money with the league, and received $34,899,808, the lowest figure in the filing that year. Counting it would put the 2020-21 spread at $3,233,907; it's left out so that every year compares the same fourteen schools.

Then $12,314,878, more than three times the widest of the four before it.

2024-25 is the first year of the success initiative, the ACC policy that lets schools keep the postseason money their own teams generate. The policy changed, and the filing changed with it, in one year.

## The success initiative reconciles the filing

The best evidence in this piece doesn't come from the conference. It comes from Virginia Tech.

In August 2025, WRAL rebuilt the ACC's first-year success-initiative payments school by school from the Virginia Tech board presentation and Armwood's comments. The schedule:

- $4,000,000 for a College Football Playoff berth
- $1,800,000 for finishing in the AP Top 25
- $1,800,000 for reaching a bowl
- about $350,000 per men's NCAA tournament game and $60,000 per women's game

Itemized totals for the fourteen full-share schools: Clemson $7,950,000, Duke $3,790,000, Syracuse and Miami $3,600,000 each, North Carolina $2,680,000, Louisville $2,270,000, NC State $1,980,000, Georgia Tech $1,860,000, Pittsburgh, Boston College, and Virginia Tech $1,800,000 each, Florida State $120,000, and Wake Forest and Virginia nothing.

Subtract each school's success payment from its filed distribution and what's left should be the base share every member gets.

| Step | Top-to-bottom spread |
| --- | --- |
| As filed, 2024-25 | $12,314,878 |
| Minus itemized success payments | $4,364,878 |
| Also minus Clemson's $3,000,000 playoff travel allowance | $3,034,822 |
| For reference, 2020-21 through 2023-24 | $2,815,267 to $3,555,690 |

After both adjustments the spread lands inside the four-year band. Syracuse is on top of the adjusted table and Wake Forest is on the bottom, and the distance between them is $3,034,822, wider than 2020-21 and narrower than the three years after it. The policy accounts for the entire break.

That second adjustment needs its label. After removing success payments, Clemson's remainder still sits $2,674,361 above the median of the other thirteen schools, and nothing on the itemized schedule accounts for it. Clemson played one playoff game, and a $3,000,000 travel allowance for that game is the only figure in view that closes the gap; take it out and Clemson drops into the pack. So I'm concluding the travel allowance is inside the filed number, the same way Part 2 found the SEC's inside Texas and Oklahoma's. That's an inference from reconciliation. The filing doesn't disclose a travel line and I haven't seen one.

Which is also the caveat on the whole table. The itemized schedule is WRAL's reconstruction from two schools' documents, not something the ACC put out. The reason to trust it is that it reconciles the filing to within the prior four years' band; the reason to say where it came from is the same.

## It was never only the playoff

With the schedule in hand, the spread among schools that didn't make the playoff stops being a mystery.

Syracuse finished second in distributions at $49,448,990 without a playoff game, because a Top 25 finish and a bowl were worth $1,800,000 apiece. Miami collected the same $3,600,000 for the same two things. Duke's $3,790,000 was mostly basketball, five men's NCAA tournament games on the way to the national semifinal.

The success initiative pays for football rankings, bowl eligibility, and basketball tournament runs. The playoff is just the largest single line on the schedule.

## Three schools got less in a record year

This is the section I didn't expect to write, and it's the one that makes the policy legible.

Year-over-year change, 2023-24 to 2024-25, full-share schools:

| School | 2023-24 | 2024-25 | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clemson | $45,274,911 | $55,129,046 | +$9,854,135 |
| Syracuse | $45,198,447 | $49,448,990 | +$4,250,543 |
| Pittsburgh | $43,363,450 | $46,304,685 | +$2,941,235 |
| Miami | $45,660,955 | $48,250,645 | +$2,589,690 |
| North Carolina | $45,349,089 | $47,916,855 | +$2,567,766 |
| Boston College | $44,597,388 | $47,087,682 | +$2,490,294 |
| Duke | $45,874,859 | $48,236,296 | +$2,361,437 |
| Virginia Tech | $44,708,872 | $46,546,737 | +$1,837,865 |
| Georgia Tech | $44,421,017 | $46,052,588 | +$1,631,571 |
| Louisville | $46,415,388 | $47,381,878 | +$966,490 |
| NC State | $45,509,841 | $46,461,278 | +$951,437 |
| Virginia | $43,879,142 | $43,626,888 | -$252,254 |
| Wake Forest | $43,072,249 | $42,814,168 | -$258,081 |
| Florida State | $46,311,450 | $43,601,179 | -$2,710,271 |

Median change: +$2,099,651.

The ACC set a revenue record in this fiscal year, and three schools received less than the year before anyway. Virginia and Wake Forest each lost about a quarter of a million dollars. Florida State lost $2,710,271, which is more than the median school gained.

Florida State went 2-10 in 2024, missed a bowl, and collected $120,000 from the success initiative, all of it from women's basketball. The year before, Florida State went 13-0 and played in the Orange Bowl, so its 2023-24 figure carried bowl money the 2024-25 figure doesn't. That's arithmetic, and it explains a meaningful part of the drop.

It doesn't explain all of it. After removing success payments, Florida State's remainder is still $1,096,486 below the median. Florida State also spent this fiscal year as a litigant against the conference, and the settlement changed how distributions are calculated. The filing doesn't separate a missing bowl from a settled lawsuit, and I'm not going to do it for the filing. Both explanations are on the table. I'm picking neither.

Now the judgment, labeled as one. A pass-through model isn't extra money for winners. It's the same pool, cut differently, and somebody funds the premium. In the ACC's first year of trying it, the people who funded it were Florida State, Wake Forest, and Virginia.

## The ones outside the comparison

Short section, because the data is thin.

SMU received $17,065,303, having agreed to forgo all ACC media revenue during its early membership. Sources disagree on how long: the dataset note says nine years, WRAL says twelve. I'm stating the conflict rather than resolving it. SMU collected $7,600,000 from the success initiative, the second-largest payment in the conference, which is about 45% of everything SMU received, and it made the playoff and lost in the first round. Unlike Texas and Oklahoma in Part 2, there's no twin school on identical terms to set SMU against, so this isn't the clean natural experiment that one was.

California received $22,993,712 and Stanford $19,557,463 on reduced entry shares. WRAL corrected its own reporting on the share to 33% of media rights, not 30%; the published dataset's note still reads 30% and will be corrected to match.

Notre Dame received $18,142,243 as a member outside football. Its NBC money doesn't reach a conference filing, apart from the 2020 season noted above.

None of these belong in a comparison against the median, for the same reason the asterisked schools didn't in Part 1.

## What this predicts

Two predictions, both checkable against the next filing, which covers the fiscal year ending June 30, 2026.

**Miami.** Miami reached the national championship game in the 2025 playoff. Under a pass-through model the prize money alone is $4,000,000 for the bid, $4,000,000 for the quarterfinal, $6,000,000 for the semifinal, and $6,000,000 for the title game, which comes to $20,000,000, before any travel allowance. If the ACC passes prize money through the way this piece describes, Miami's next distribution should clear the conference median by at least that much, and the spread should make 2024-25 look narrow. If Miami's premium comes in well short of $20,000,000, the pass-through description is wrong or incomplete, and I'll say so here.

**The viewership model.** The ACC has also moved to distributing 60% of its television revenue on five-year viewership figures, with the remaining 40% split evenly. That change postdates this filing. The next return is the first one that can show it, and it should widen the spread on its own, regardless of what happens on the field.

## Where this leaves the series

Three conferences, three answers to the same question.

The Big Ten passes playoff money to the school that earned it, and the gap opened. The SEC appears to pool it, and its fourteen full members finished within $4,116,525 of each other. The ACC passes it through, and its spread more than tripled in a single year.

Same playoff, same money. What a school receives depends less on what it did than on which conference it did it in.
