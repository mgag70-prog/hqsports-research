# Article outline: the ACC, Part 3 of the conference distributions series

Part 1 was the Big Ten and the twelve-team playoff opening a gap inside a
conference. Part 2 was the SEC, where the same mechanism produced almost no
divergence and the best explanation was pooling. Part 3 is the control case:
the conference that passes the money straight through, and what that did to
its filing in the first year it tried.

Every number here is from the dataset already published with Parts 1 and 2.
No new source is required for the core finding.

## Working titles

1. The ACC paid its schools within $3.6 million of each other for four years.
   Then it started paying them for winning.
2. The SEC pooled its playoff money. The ACC passed it through. Three schools
   got less in a record year.
3. Strip out what the ACC paid for winning and it is an equal-share conference
   again. Almost to the dollar.

Number 1 is the finding. Number 2 is the one that ties the series together.

## Section 2. Four flat years

The setup, and it is the whole reason the fifth year matters.

Top-to-bottom spread among ACC full-share schools:

| Season | Schools | Top | Bottom | Spread |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2020-21 | 15 | $38,133,715 | $34,899,808 | $3,233,907 |
| 2021-22 | 14 | $41,282,420 | $37,961,705 | $3,320,715 |
| 2022-23 | 14 | $46,850,044 | $43,294,354 | $3,555,690 |
| 2023-24 | 14 | $46,415,388 | $43,072,249 | $3,343,139 |
| 2024-25 | 14 | $55,129,046 | $42,814,168 | $12,314,878 |

Four years inside a band of roughly $3.3 to $3.6 million, which is what an
equal-shares conference looks like in a filing. Then the fifth year is
$12,314,878, nearly four times the widest of the four before it.

2024-25 is the first year of the success initiative, the ACC policy that lets
schools keep the postseason money their own teams generate. The policy changed
and the filing changed with it, in one year, by a factor of almost four.

## Section 3. The success initiative reconciles the filing

The best evidence in this piece does not come from the conference. It comes
from Virginia Tech.

In August 2025, WRAL rebuilt the ACC's first-year success-initiative payments
school by school from a Virginia Tech Board of Visitors presentation and
comments by NC State's chief financial officer, Beverly Armwood, at a
February 6, 2025 trustees meeting. The schedule:

- $4,000,000 for a College Football Playoff berth
- $1,800,000 for finishing in the AP Top 25
- $1,800,000 for reaching a bowl
- about $350,000 per men's NCAA tournament game, $60,000 per women's

Itemized totals for full-share schools: Clemson $7,950,000, Duke $3,790,000,
Syracuse and Miami $3,600,000 each, North Carolina $2,680,000, Louisville
$2,270,000, NC State $1,980,000, Georgia Tech $1,860,000, Pittsburgh, Boston
College, and Virginia Tech $1,800,000 each, Florida State $120,000, and Wake
Forest and Virginia nothing.

Now subtract each school's success payment from its filed distribution. What
is left should be the equal base share every member gets.

| Step | Top-to-bottom spread |
| --- | --- |
| As filed, 2024-25 | $12,314,878 |
| Minus itemized success payments | $4,364,878 |
| Also minus Clemson's $3,000,000 playoff travel allowance | $3,034,822 |
| For reference, 2020-21 through 2023-24 | $3,233,907 to $3,555,690 |

After both adjustments the ACC looks like an equal-share conference again,
sitting right back in the band it occupied for four straight years. The policy
explains the entire break.

**This answers the travel question, and label the answer as inference.** After
removing success payments, Clemson's remainder is still $2,674,361 above the
median of the other thirteen. Nothing in the itemized schedule accounts for
that except the $3,000,000 travel allowance Clemson received for its one
playoff game, and removing it closes the gap. The travel allowance is almost
certainly inside the filed figure, the same way Part 2 found it inside the
SEC's. That is a conclusion from reconciliation, not a line the filing
discloses. Say it that way.

Caveat worth stating: the itemized schedule is WRAL's reconstruction from
institutional documents, not a table the ACC published. The fact that it
reconciles the filing to within the prior four years' band is the reason to
trust it, and also the reason to say where it came from.

## Section 4. It was never only the playoff

With the itemized schedule in hand, the non-playoff spread stops being a
mystery. Syracuse finished second in distributions at $49,448,990 without a
playoff trip because it collected $3,600,000 for a Top 25 finish and a bowl.
Miami got the same $3,600,000. Duke's $3,790,000 was mostly basketball: five
games in the men's NCAA tournament, reaching the national semifinal.

So the success initiative pays for football rankings, bowl eligibility, and
basketball tournament runs, not just the College Football Playoff. The
playoff is simply the biggest single payment on the schedule.

## Section 5. Three schools got less in a record year

This is the part that makes the policy legible, and it is the section I did
not expect to write.

Year over year change, 2023-24 to 2024-25, full-share schools:

| School | 2023-24 | 2024-25 | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clemson | $45,274,911 | $55,129,046 | +$9,854,135 |
| Syracuse | $45,198,447 | $49,448,990 | +$4,250,543 |
| Pittsburgh | $43,363,450 | $46,304,685 | +$2,941,235 |
| Miami | $45,660,955 | $48,250,645 | +$2,589,690 |
| North Carolina | $45,349,089 | $47,916,855 | +$2,567,766 |
| Boston College | $44,597,388 | $47,087,682 | +$2,490,294 |
| Duke | $45,874,859 | $48,236,296 | +$2,361,437 |
| Virginia Tech | $44,708,872 | $46,546,737 | +$1,837,865 |
| Georgia Tech | $44,421,017 | $46,052,588 | +$1,631,571 |
| Louisville | $46,415,388 | $47,381,878 | +$966,490 |
| NC State | $45,509,841 | $46,461,278 | +$951,437 |
| Virginia | $43,879,142 | $43,626,888 | -$252,254 |
| Wake Forest | $43,072,249 | $42,814,168 | -$258,081 |
| Florida State | $46,311,450 | $43,601,179 | -$2,710,271 |

Median change: +$2,099,651.

The ACC set a revenue record in this fiscal year. Three schools received less
money than the year before anyway, and Florida State lost $2,710,271, which is
more than the entire top-to-bottom spread of any prior year in this dataset.

Florida State went 2-10 in 2024, missed a bowl, and collected $120,000 from
the success initiative, all of it from women's basketball. The year before,
Florida State went 13-0 and played in the Orange Bowl, so its 2023-24 figure
carried bowl money its 2024-25 figure did not. That explains a meaningful part
of the drop with arithmetic.

It does not explain all of it: after removing success payments, Florida
State's remainder is still about $1,096,486 below the median. Florida State
was also a litigant against the conference, and the settlement changed
distribution formulas. The filing does not separate those explanations, so
name both and pick neither.

**The point, stated as a judgment.** A pass-through model is not extra money
for winners. It is the same pool, cut differently. Somebody funds the premium,
and in the ACC's first year of trying it, the people who funded it were
Florida State, Wake Forest, and Virginia.

## Section 6. The ones outside the comparison

Short section, because the data is thin.

- SMU: $17,065,303, having agreed to forgo all ACC media revenue during its
  early membership. Sources conflict on the length: the dataset note says nine
  years, WRAL says twelve. Resolve before publishing or state the conflict.
  SMU collected $7,600,000 from the success initiative, the second-largest
  payment in the conference, which is about 45% of everything SMU received. SMU also made the playoff and lost in the first round. Unlike Texas
  and Oklahoma in Part 2, there is no twin school on identical terms to
  compare against, so this is not the clean natural experiment that one was.
  Say so rather than stretching it.
- California $22,993,712 and Stanford $19,557,463, on reduced entry shares.
  WRAL corrected its own reporting to 33% of media rights, not 30%; the
  published dataset's note says 30% and should be corrected to match.
- Notre Dame $18,142,243, a member outside football, whose NBC money never
  appears in a conference filing.

None of these belong in a comparison against the median, for the same reason
the asterisked schools did not in Part 1.

## Section 7. What this predicts

Two predictions, both checkable against the next filing, covering the fiscal
year ending June 30, 2026.

**Miami.** Miami reached the national championship game in the 2025 playoff.
Under a pass-through model the prize money alone is $4,000,000 for the bid,
$4,000,000 for the quarterfinal, $6,000,000 for the semifinal, and $6,000,000
for the title game, which is $20,000,000, before any travel allowance. If the
ACC passes prize money through as described, Miami's next distribution should
clear the conference median by at least that much, and the ACC's spread should
make 2024-25 look narrow. If Miami's premium comes in far below $20,000,000,
the pass-through description is wrong or incomplete and I will say so here.

**The viewership model.** The ACC has also moved to distributing 60% of its
television revenue on five-year viewership figures, with the remaining 40%
split evenly. That change postdates this filing. The next return is the first
one that can show it, and it should widen the spread independently of anything
that happens on the field.

Verify Miami's exact number of playoff games before publishing, since the
travel figure depends on it and the prize figure does not.

## Section 8. Where this leaves the series

Three conferences, three different answers to the same question.

The Big Ten passes playoff money to the school that earned it and the gap
opened. The SEC appears to pool it and its fourteen full members finished
within $4,116,525 of each other. The ACC passes it through and its spread
nearly quadrupled in a single year.

Same playoff, same money, three policies, three filings that look nothing
alike. What a school receives depends less on what it did than on which
conference it did it in.
