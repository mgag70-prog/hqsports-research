# In the Big Ten, money explains the top three teams and the bottom one. For the other fourteen it explains nothing.

*Illinois has the fourth-best record in the Big Ten and the fourth-cheapest roster.*

---

*Construction note, before any finding. The roster budgets come from The Athletic's report of September 16, 2026, by Stewart Mandel, David Ubben, Sam Khan Jr. and the Athletic Colleges Team, built from more than 70 sources with stated bias corrections: coaches undersell, administrators oversell, and rivals inflate each other. Every budget The Athletic published is a range. Every midpoint here is mine, calculated from the range. The records are Sports Reference conference standings for 2024 and 2025 combined, bowl and playoff games included. Payroll rank is by midpoint across the 18 Big Ten programs.*

*The Power 4 record is a secondary cut of the same two seasons, computed game by game rather than taken from a source, and the definition changes the answer, so here it is. Power 4 means opponents from the ACC, Big Ten, Big 12, and SEC, plus Notre Dame. Conference championship games and College Football Playoff games against those opponents count. Games against Oregon State and Washington State don't, because neither school plays in a Power 4 conference after realignment. Different choices would produce a different table; these are stated so you can disagree with them rather than guess at them.*

*Ties are shared. Iowa and Michigan both went 17-9, so both are sixth and nobody is seventh. Indiana and Michigan both sit at $36-40 million, so both are fourth in payroll. Every rank gap in this piece is payroll rank minus record rank using those shared ranks. Where a table in the repository breaks a tie for sorting purposes, the piece doesn't.*

*And the timing problem, which is the biggest caveat and so goes first. These are 2026 roster budgets set against 2024 and 2025 results. Revenue sharing began in July 2025, which means that for most of the measured window the system that produced these budgets didn't exist. The claim isn't that 2026 money bought 2024 wins. The claim is narrower and, I think, more useful: the conference's current payroll order and its recent results order agree at the top, agree at the bottom, and agree nowhere in between. Whether money causes wins, or wins attract money, or both run on some third thing like a coach who stays, is left open on purpose.*

## The correlation, and where it dies

The arithmetic first. All correlations are Spearman rank correlations between budget midpoint and winning percentage, with tied values given their average rank.

- Across all 18 teams, payroll against two-year winning percentage comes out at 0.472. Positive, real, and modest.
- Remove the top three by record and Purdue, and the same test on the remaining fourteen returns -0.071. That's nothing. In a sample of fourteen, a figure that close to zero on either side means the same thing.
- Power 4 games only, the same two tests: 0.469 across all 18, and -0.075 for the middle fourteen. Taking the cupcakes out doesn't rescue the relationship; it's the same shape with the same hole in the middle.

The same thing in tiers, with one wrinkle I'd rather show than hide. Iowa and Michigan have identical 17-9 records and one of them has to be the sixth team when you cut the table into thirds. With Michigan sixth, the top six by record average $38.3 million on midpoint, the middle six $25.8 million, and the bottom six $24.0 million. Put Iowa sixth instead and the top six average drops to $35.2 million and the middle six rises to $29.0 million, because you've swapped a $38.0 million roster for a $19.0 million one across the line. The bottom six stay at $24.0 million either way.

What doesn't move under either ordering is the wins. The middle six averaged 15.0 wins over the two years and the bottom six averaged 8.0, a gap of 3.5 wins a season, and on the Michigan-sixth cut the two tiers spent within $1.8 million of each other to produce it. The gap in payroll that matters in this conference is between the top six and everyone else, not between the ninth-best team and the seventeenth.

Now the argument, which is a judgment and not a measurement. The Big Ten doesn't have a spending-to-winning relationship. It has a threshold at the top, where the three best records belong to three of the four richest rosters; a floor at the bottom, where Purdue sits alone on both axes; and fourteen programs in between whose payrolls tell you nothing about their records. What reads as a league-wide correlation of 0.472 is three rich programs and one poor one doing all of the work, and when you take those four out of the sample the number collapses to zero.

## The programs that beat their payroll

Rank gap is payroll rank minus record rank, on shared ranks. Positive means the team finished higher than it spent.

| Team | Record rank | P4 rank | Payroll rank | Gap (record) | Gap (P4) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Illinois | 4 | 4 | 15 | +11 | +11 |
| Iowa | T6 | T6 | 17 | +11 | +11 |
| Minnesota | T8 | 8 | 16 | +8 | +8 |

Three of the four cheapest rosters in the Big Ten are in the top eight by record and the top eight against Power 4 opponents. The fourth is Purdue.

One check before the cases, and it's a check that came back clean rather than a finding. The gaps barely move between the two cuts. Illinois is +11 overall and +11 on Power 4 games, Iowa is +11 and +11, Minnesota is +8 and +8, and in the other direction Nebraska is -5 and -5. The first objection a reader would raise is that the cheap overperformers padded their records against soft non-conference schedules; if that were the mechanism, stripping those games out would shrink the gaps. It doesn't.

Lead with the tie. Illinois and Iowa share the largest gap in the conference at +11, and they hold it on both cuts of the data, overall and Power 4. They get there two different ways. Illinois is a program that wasn't good and now is. Iowa is a program that's been the same thing for 27 years. Both beat their payroll by the same margin, which is the reason to treat this as a pattern with at least two members rather than a story about one coach having a good stretch.

Illinois gets the most room, because the change is the point. 19-7 over two seasons is a program record across 133 years of Illinois football, with back-to-back nine-win seasons for the first time. Bielema inherited a program that had gone 17-39 under Lovie Smith from 2016 through 2020; he's 38-26 at Illinois, the second-best winning percentage in school history among coaches with at least 50 games. Iowa and Minnesota are holding a level. Illinois built one, and did it on the 15th-largest payroll in an 18-team league.

When Bielema was asked about The Athletic putting him fourth-lowest at $19-23 million, he said he'd been told the report was out but hadn't read it, so he didn't know whether it was factual. That's the estimate caveat delivered by the subject of the estimate, and it's worth more than any hedge I could write for myself. His other claims check out against the data. Fourth-lowest by midpoint is right: Purdue at $18.0 million, Iowa at $19.0 million, Minnesota at $20.0 million, then Illinois at $21.0 million. "Half the Big Ten is below $25 million" is exactly nine of 18 on midpoint. One correction: he grouped Nebraska with the $35 million-plus tier, and Nebraska is $31-35 million, midpoint $33.0 million, which is sixth in the conference but not in that tier.

Iowa is the stronger case, and it gets equal room. 17-9 on the second-cheapest roster in the conference, level with Michigan and ahead of USC, Washington, and Nebraska over two years, in Kirk Ferentz's 26th and 27th seasons. Illinois's run is new. Iowa's is the same thing it's been doing since 1999, which makes it the better test of the question that matters here: whether a cheap roster can hold a position over time rather than spike for two years and fall back. Illinois can't answer that yet. Iowa has been answering it for a quarter century.

What Bielema says the difference is, quoted and not endorsed. Players taking less to stay, and he named Te'Rah Edwards and an unnamed receiver, who I believe is Collin Dixon, he says had offers for more money elsewhere. Josh Whitman's support as athletic director. And a line about trying to play by rules that he says don't always get obeyed nationally, which is an allegation with nobody named in it. I'm quoting it because he said it, noting that it's unverifiable as stated, and building nothing on it.

Minnesota is the third case and shouldn't be left as a table row. 16-10 overall, eighth in Power 4 winning percentage, 16th of 18 in payroll, a gap of +8 on both cuts. P.J. Fleck is in his tenth season and is the second-longest-tenured active coach in the Big Ten behind Ferentz. Minnesota went 8-5 in 2024 and 8-5 in 2025, which is about as literal a definition of holding a level as the data can offer.

Fleck responded to the same Athletic report Bielema was asked about, on his radio show, and his quote is the Minnesota version of the same argument: if you're not one of the teams with an accelerated amount of money, like the New York Yankees, you have to get creative quickly and develop freshmen quickly. He also described spending most of the roster budget on retention, naming the players he wanted to keep. That's the same mechanism Bielema describes, from a different program, unprompted, in the same week. Two coaches saying the same thing isn't evidence that it's true. It is worth putting side by side.

The pattern worth naming, and it's a hypothesis rather than a finding. The three programs that most outperform their payroll have the three longest coaching tenures among them: Ferentz in his 27th season, Fleck in his tenth, Bielema in his sixth. The programs that most underperform it have all changed coaches recently or are in the middle of doing so. Three teams isn't a mechanism, and continuity could as easily be a result of winning cheaply as a cause of it; a coach who wins on a small budget doesn't get fired, so tenure and overperformance would travel together whichever way the arrow points. Say that, then put it on the table anyway, because it's the only thing the three cheap overperformers visibly share.

USC leads this section even though it isn't the worst gap, because of the scale involved. Third-largest payroll in the Big Ten at $37-40 million, tied for eighth in record and ninth against Power 4 opponents, and the only one of the conference's three richest rosters that isn't in the top three on either table. Nebraska is sixth in payroll and eleventh in record on both cuts, and hasn't posted a winning Big Ten record under Matt Rhule. Wisconsin at $27-31 million is the eighth-largest payroll in the conference and tied for 14th in record. UCLA, one slot below it in payroll at $26-30 million, is tied for 16th, the worst record of any program above the $25 million line.

Maryland, Michigan State, Washington, and Northwestern all sit on the same $21-25 million range and share the tenth payroll rank. Washington turned that into the tenth-best record, exactly what it paid for. Northwestern finished 13th, Michigan State tied for 14th, and Maryland tied for 16th on the same money, which is one more small illustration of the middle of this conference not sorting by payroll.

## The firings came from the middle

Four Big Ten programs changed head coaches out of the 2025 season. One has to be excluded, and the reason stated: Michigan parted with Sherrone Moore for cause, over what the school described as credible evidence of an inappropriate relationship with a staff member, not over performance. Michigan's 17-9 and tied-for-sixth record doesn't belong in a spending-and-results pattern about firings, and it isn't in the table below.

The three performance firings:

| Program | Payroll rank | Record rank | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Penn State | 7 | 5 | Fired James Franklin on October 12, 2025, after an 0-3 Big Ten start; owed close to $50 million |
| UCLA | 9 | T16 | Fired DeShaun Foster three weeks into the 2025 season at 0-3 |
| Michigan St. | T10 | T14 | Fired Jonathan Smith after two seasons; 9-15 overall across 2024 and 2025 |

None of the three is a bottom-tier spender. Purdue, Iowa, and Minnesota, the three cheapest rosters in the conference, all kept their coaches. Purdue kept Barry Odom after his first season in 2025, and Iowa and Minnesota kept Ferentz and Fleck while finishing tied for sixth and tied for eighth. The programs that decided their results weren't good enough were all in the middle of the payroll table, seventh through tenth, and one of them was fifth in the standings.

Penn State is the sharpest single fact in the piece. Franklin's teams went 20-9 across 2024 and 2025, fifth-best in the Big Ten, and that stretch included a 13-3 season and a College Football Playoff semifinal. He was fired seven games into 2025 and, per the Associated Press on October 12, 2025, owed close to $50 million. Penn State's entire 2026 roster costs $29-32 million.

The buyout exceeded the payroll. A program committed more money to stop employing one coach than it committed to paying every player on its 2026 team, and it did so after two years that ranked fifth in an 18-team conference.

That comparison is a judgment, not an accounting identity. Buyouts and roster budgets come out of different accounts on different schedules, the buyout is spread over years while the roster figure is a single season, and "close to $50 million" is an announced figure rather than an audited one. I'm making the comparison anyway because the scale is real and the order of magnitude doesn't depend on the accounting: close to $50 million against a roster of $29-32 million is a gap of somewhere between $18 million and $21 million, and the larger figure bought nothing.

## What would make this wrong

Each of these is live, and any one of them could turn out to matter more than the tables above.

The budgets are survey estimates, not filings. Nobody signed them. The coach at the center of this piece says he hasn't read the report and can't confirm his own number, and I have no way to check it that he doesn't.

The timing mismatch from the construction note, restated plainly: two of the four team-seasons in the record column predate revenue sharing, which started in July 2025. The 2026 budgets describe a system that was in place for at most one of the two measured seasons. If the payroll order in 2026 looks materially different from what programs were spending through collectives in 2024, the comparison is between two different things.

Two years is short. Illinois's 19-7 is a program record across 133 years, which is another way of saying it isn't the normal state of the program, and a piece built partly on Illinois should say so about Illinois.

Expectation predicted poorly, and this is a form of expectation. The 2025 preseason Big Ten media poll picked Penn State first, Ohio State second, Oregon third, Illinois fourth, Michigan fifth, and Indiana sixth. Indiana won the national title. Penn State fired its coach in October. Anyone who'd read the payroll table as a forecast in August 2025 would have had the bottom of the top six upside down, and that cuts against reading too much into any snapshot, this one included.

A rank correlation on 18 teams is a small sample, and one on 14 is smaller. The -0.071 figure for the middle fourteen is the absence of a relationship in this data. It's not proof that no relationship exists, and a different two-year window could produce a different number.

Bielema's account of how the roster was built, players taking less to stay and the rest, is unverifiable from outside the program. Fleck's is the same. Two coaches describing the same mechanism the same week is a coincidence and nothing more until someone with access to the numbers checks it.

## What this predicts

A reading of the data that can't be wrong isn't worth much, so here is what the threshold reading commits to for the 2026 season.

If it's right, the three top-payroll programs should stay at or near the top of the conference in 2026 and Purdue should stay at the bottom, while the middle fourteen reshuffle in a way payroll doesn't predict. Illinois, Iowa, and Minnesota should not collapse toward their spending rank; at least two of the three should finish in the top half of the conference again on rosters ranked 15th, 17th, and 16th.

If instead the middle sorts itself by payroll this season, with Michigan, USC, and Nebraska rising toward their spending and Illinois, Iowa, and Minnesota falling toward theirs, then the pattern in these tables was two years of noise and the national version of the argument, that money explains the top and little else, was wrong at conference scale.

Revisit this at the end of the 2026 season and say which one happened.

## The data

Budgets are in the committed roster budgets CSV from the first piece: school, conference, low and high estimate in millions, midpoint, and AP rank as of September 13, 2026, for 68 teams. A second file in this piece's data folder carries the 18 Big Ten programs with both seasons' records, the two-year combined record and winning percentage, the Power 4 record and winning percentage, the budget range and midpoint, each rank, and the two gap columns.

Two notes on that second file. Its rank columns break ties by position for sorting, so Iowa is listed sixth and Michigan seventh, Minnesota eighth and USC ninth, and the four programs on the $21-25 million range are listed 10 through 13 in payroll. The piece uses shared ranks instead, and the gap figures in the text are computed from the shared ranks, so they differ from the file's gap columns for tied teams. The correlations in the text are Spearman rank correlations on budget midpoint against winning percentage, with tied values assigned their average rank; recomputing with a different tie rule will move the figures at the second decimal and not the conclusion.

Coaching changes aren't in either dataset. Franklin's firing date and the buyout figure are from the Associated Press, October 12, 2025. The Moore language is from Michigan's own statement. The Foster and Smith firings are from contemporaneous reporting at the time of each dismissal.

Power 4, restated so anyone recomputing it can match the method: opponents from the ACC, Big Ten, Big 12, and SEC, plus Notre Dame. Conference championship games and College Football Playoff games against those opponents count. Oregon State and Washington State don't count. Sources for the underlying schedules and results are Sports Reference, 2024 and 2025 seasons.
