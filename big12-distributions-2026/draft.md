# The ACC started paying its schools for winning. The Big 12 just stopped.

*Part 4 of the distributions series, and a correction to what I said about the Big Ten.*

---

*Construction note, before anything else. The Big 12 numbers here come from the conference's Form 990 for the fiscal year ending June 30, 2025, retrieved through ProPublica by the same method as [Part 1](https://www.gridironhq.ai/blog/college-football-playoff-conference-revenue-sharing), [Part 2](https://www.gridironhq.ai/blog/sec-playoff-distribution-revenue-sharing), and [Part 3](https://ledgerandwhistle.substack.com/p/the-acc-paid-its-schools-within-36). The Big Ten numbers that carry the correction later in the piece were already in that dataset. Nothing new was added.*

*Fiscal years don't line up. The Big 12 and the Big Ten close June 30; the SEC closes August 31. A row labeled 2024-25 covers a different window in each conference, but every window here contains the 2024 season and its playoff.*

*One rule holds throughout. When this piece says what a conference's policy is, that comes from named reporting, Forbes and Business of College Sports. When it says what a filing shows, that's the return. The two never get blended, because the filings don't describe policy and the reporting doesn't audit the filings.*

## The Big 12 was never an equal-share conference

That's a judgment, and I'll show the work for it. The common description of the Big 12 is that it splits playoff money equally. In the year covered by this filing it didn't, and it hadn't for a while.

The twelve full-share distributions for 2024-25, from the return:

| School | 2024-25 |
| --- | --- |
| Arizona State | $43,009,550 |
| Iowa State | $41,194,426 |
| Baylor | $39,950,085 |
| Kansas State | $39,830,544 |
| Texas Tech | $39,734,106 |
| West Virginia | $39,582,600 |
| TCU | $39,272,007 |
| Colorado | $39,034,422 |
| Kansas | $38,312,680 |
| Oklahoma State | $38,038,756 |
| Arizona | $38,009,311 |
| Utah | $37,879,865 |

Top to bottom is $5,129,685. Take the top two out and the remaining ten sit within $2,070,220 of each other, which is roughly what an equal-share conference looks like on a Form 990. The four newcomers from the Pac-12 are on full shares in their first year, so they belong in the twelve; BYU, Cincinnati, UCF, and Houston are on entry-agreement shares and don't.

The two schools above the pack are the two that went somewhere. Arizona State won the Big 12, took a first-round bye, and played one playoff game, a quarterfinal. It came in $3,856,336 above the median of the other ten. Iowa State lost the conference title game and won its bowl, and came in $2,041,212 above.

The mechanism isn't in the filing. Business of College Sports and Forbes both describe the Big 12 as having compensated playoff participants through a formula: at least $1,900,000 plus travel for each of the first two rounds, $2,200,000 plus travel for the semifinal and the championship. Arizona State's premium is consistent with one game under that schedule. Consistent is the right word and proof is the wrong one, because the return doesn't itemize postseason money and $3,856,336 isn't a figure the reported formula produces exactly.

## The pattern goes back further than the playoff

One year is an observation. Five is a policy.

Full-share spreads by season, and the school on top:

| Season | Schools | Spread | Top |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2020-21 | 10 | $1,763,326 | Oklahoma |
| 2021-22 | 10 | $2,858,334 | Oklahoma State and Oklahoma, tied |
| 2022-23 | 10 | $6,273,119 | TCU |
| 2023-24 | 10 | $4,277,657 | Texas |
| 2024-25 | 12 | $5,129,685 | Arizona State |

The widest year isn't the playoff year. It's 2022-23, when TCU reached the national championship game and received $48,258,005 in a conference where the next school got $45,195,567.

A different school leads almost every season, and each time it's a school that did something in the postseason. Read one year and you might call it a wobble; read five and it's a conference that has paid for performance since at least 2020-21 while carrying a reputation as the one that splits everything.

## And then it stopped

Beginning in 2025-26, the Big 12 divides all playoff revenue equally among its members. Forbes reported the change in January 2026 and illustrated it with Texas Tech's playoff run, which under the new rule generates the same money for Oklahoma State as for Texas Tech.

Set that beside Part 3. The ACC introduced its success initiative in 2024-25, the first year it paid schools to keep what their own postseason generated, and its full-share spread went from four years inside $3,555,690 to $12,314,878. The Big 12 abolished its participation formula, and its equal split begins in 2025-26, one season after the ACC's initiative began.

Two conferences, the same expanded playoff, opposite directions. What follows is my reading, not the filings': the ACC is paying its best programs more to keep them from leaving, and the Big 12 is paying everyone the same to keep the whole membership from fracturing. A Form 990 can't tell you motive. It can tell you which way each conference moved, and they moved apart.

The next Big 12 return, for the year ending June 30, 2026, is the first one that can show the flat policy in the numbers.

## The correction

I came to these filings for the Big 12 and found the Big Ten wrong along the way. It goes here, before the four-conference summary rather than after it, because that summary depends on the Big Ten characterization being right, and the one I published in Parts 1 and 3 wasn't.

In Part 1, I wrote that the Big Ten passes playoff money to the school that earned it, and that this is what opened the gap inside the conference in 2024-25. Part 3 repeated it as settled. It's wrong, or imprecise enough to mislead, and the filing I'd already been looking at says so.

Here's what I saw. Big Ten full-share spreads, top school to bottom:

| Season | Schools | Spread |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2020-21 | 11 | $185,000 |
| 2021-22 | 11 | $175,000 |
| 2022-23 | 12 | $175,000 |
| 2023-24 | 12 | $375,001 |
| 2024-25 | 16 | $15,540,424 |

Four years inside $375,001, flatter than the ACC ever managed, and then $15,540,424. Three playoff teams sat at the top of that column, so I read the gap as prize money passing through. That reading fits the shape of the table and doesn't survive the arithmetic.

Take the thirteen full-share schools that played no playoff game and find their median: $78,057,558. Measure the three playoff schools against it.

| School | Playoff games | Premium over median | Per game |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Indiana | 1 | $2,952,339 | $2,952,339 |
| Penn State | 3 | $10,863,604 | $3,621,201 |
| Ohio State | 4 | $13,494,524 | $3,373,631 |

Indiana played one playoff game, a first-round loss, and received $2,952,339 more than a school that stayed home. The playoff pays each participant a $3,000,000 travel allowance per game. The gap between those two figures is $47,661.

If the Big Ten were passing prize money through, Indiana's line would carry $4,000,000 for the bid on top of the travel, and Ohio State's would carry far more than $13,494,524 for winning the national championship, since the prize money for that run is $20,000,000 before travel. Neither happened. What the filing supports is the policy Forbes has described as the Big Ten's long-standing one: prize money split equally across the membership, travel reimbursement passed to the school that incurred it.

What I can't explain: Penn State and Ohio State both cleared $3,000,000 per game, by $621,201 and $373,631 respectively, and Penn State's per-game figure is higher than Ohio State's despite the shorter run. Something else sits in those two numbers, and the return doesn't break it out. I'm stating that and stopping there.

(Oregon also played a playoff game, one, and is missing from the table above because it's on a partial share under its entry agreement through 2030. It isn't one of the sixteen.)

So the correction is narrow and specific. The Big Ten's 2024-25 gap is real and the size in Parts 1 and 3 is right. The cause I named was wrong. It's travel, not prize money.

## Where the series lands

Four conferences, four policies, one playoff.

**Big Ten.** Prize money split equally, travel passed through. Four years within $375,001, then a gap that reads as a travel line once you measure it per game.

**SEC.** Appears to pool. Fourteen full members finished within $4,116,525 of each other in a year when three of them made the playoff.

**ACC.** Passes postseason money through, bowls and basketball included, in its first year of trying. Spread went from four years inside $3,555,690 to $12,314,878.

**Big 12.** Ran a participation formula for at least five years, then abolished it and went equal for 2025-26.

The same twelve-team bracket sends the same money into four conference offices and gets four different answers about who keeps it. What a school receives for a playoff run depends less on the run than on which office the check lands in first.

## What would make this wrong

The Big 12 formula is reported, not filed. Arizona State's $3,856,336 premium is consistent with one game under the reported schedule and doesn't reproduce it. If the reporting is off, the consistency is a coincidence.

The correction rests on one school and one game. Indiana's single-game premium lands within $47,661 of the $3,000,000 travel allowance, and that match is doing almost all of the work. Penn State's and Ohio State's per-game figures don't land as cleanly, and I can't account for the excess. If the $3,000,000 read is coincidence, the correction is wrong too, and Parts 1 and 3 were right for reasons I still couldn't show. I don't think that's likely, but it's live.

Conference fiscal years aren't aligned, and none of these returns itemize postseason money as its own line. Every premium here is a difference between a school's total distribution and a median, not a disclosed playoff payment.

Arizona State won the Big 12 championship game, and its premium could include championship game money the filing doesn't separate from playoff money. Iowa State, which lost that game, still came in $2,041,212 above the median, so the title game alone doesn't explain the gap between the two of them, but it could inflate Arizona State's number.

## What this predicts

The Big 12 return for the year ending June 30, 2026 should show the flat policy. Full-share schools inside roughly $2,000,000 of each other, and no school pulling clear on the strength of a playoff run, despite Texas Tech's 2025 season. If a gap the size of Arizona State's shows up again, the flat policy either didn't take effect or doesn't work the way Forbes reported it.

The Big Ten return for the same year should show a premium of about $3,000,000 per playoff game for each participant and no prize-money pass-through. If a Big Ten playoff team clears the non-playoff median by far more than $3,000,000 a game, this correction needs correcting, and I'll do it here.
