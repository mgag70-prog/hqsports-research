# Article outline: the Big 12, Part 4, and a correction

The last of the four power conferences, and the piece that closes the series.
It also corrects something Parts 1 and 3 got wrong about the Big Ten, which
gets its own section rather than a footnote.

Everything runs on the distributions dataset already published with Parts 1
through 3. No new dataset.

## Working titles

1. The Big 12 paid its schools for winning, then stopped. The ACC started in
   the same year.
2. I said the Big Ten passed playoff money to the schools that earned it. The
   filing says it passed the travel.
3. Four conferences, four answers, and one of mine was wrong.

Number 1 is the finding. Number 2 is the correction and the more honest hook.
Number 3 is the series close. Pick before drafting; they lead to different
first paragraphs.

## Section 1. Construction note

- Source is the Big 12 Form 990 for the fiscal year ending June 30, 2025,
  retrieved through ProPublica, same method and same dataset as Parts 1
  through 3.
- Fiscal years are not aligned across conferences. The Big 12 closes June 30;
  the SEC closes August 31.
- Policy descriptions come from named reporting, not the filing. Forbes and
  Business of College Sports both describe the mechanisms. Where the piece
  says what a conference's policy is, that is reporting. Where it says what a
  filing shows, that is the return. Keep those separate in the prose.

## Section 2. The Big 12 was never an equal-share conference

The common description is that the Big 12 splits playoff money equally. In the
year in this filing, it did not.

2024-25 full-share distributions, twelve schools:

| School | 2024-25 |
| --- | --- |
| Arizona State | $43,009,550 |
| Iowa State | $41,194,426 |
| Baylor | $39,950,085 |
| Kansas State | $39,830,544 |
| Texas Tech | $39,734,106 |
| West Virginia | $39,582,600 |
| TCU | $39,272,007 |
| Colorado | $39,034,422 |
| Kansas | $38,312,680 |
| Oklahoma State | $38,038,756 |
| Arizona | $38,009,311 |
| Utah | $37,879,865 |

Top to bottom is $5,129,685. But take the top two out and the remaining ten
sit within **$2,070,220** of each other, which is what an equal-share
conference looks like.

- Arizona State won the Big 12 and played one playoff game, a quarterfinal
  after a first-round bye. It came in **$3,856,336** above the median of those
  ten.
- Iowa State lost the conference title game and won its bowl. It came in
  **$2,041,212** above.

The mechanism is reported, not disclosed in the filing. Business of College
Sports and Forbes both describe the Big 12 as having compensated playoff
participants through a formula paying at least $1,900,000 plus travel for the
first two rounds and $2,200,000 plus travel for the semifinal and
championship. Arizona State's premium is consistent with one game under that
formula. Label the match as consistency, not proof: the filing does not
itemize, and $3,856,336 is not a number the reported formula produces exactly.

## Section 3. The pattern goes back further than the playoff

This is what stops the piece being a one-year observation.

Full-share spreads by year, and the school on top:

| Season | Schools | Spread | Top |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2020-21 | 10 | $1,763,326 | Oklahoma |
| 2021-22 | 10 | $2,858,334 | Oklahoma State |
| 2022-23 | 10 | $6,273,119 | TCU |
| 2023-24 | 10 | $4,277,657 | Texas |
| 2024-25 | 12 | $5,129,685 | Arizona State |

The widest year is 2022-23, before the playoff expanded, and the school on top
is TCU, which reached the national championship game that season at
$48,258,005 against a conference where the next school was $45,195,567.

A different school leads almost every year, and in each case it is a school
that went somewhere in the postseason. This is not an equal-share conference
that occasionally wobbles. It is a conference that has been paying for
performance for at least five years, quietly, while being described as the one
that splits everything.

## Section 4. And then it stopped

The turn, and the reason to write this now.

Beginning in 2025-26, the Big 12 divides all playoff revenue equally among its
members. Reported by Forbes in January 2026, and illustrated there with Texas
Tech's playoff run generating the same money for Oklahoma State as for Texas
Tech itself.

Put that next to Part 3. The ACC introduced its success initiative in
2024-25, the first year it paid schools to keep what their own postseason
generated. In the same window, the Big 12 abolished its participation formula
and went flat.

Two conferences, facing the same expanded playoff, moved in opposite
directions. Label the reading as a judgment: the ACC is trying to keep its
best programs from leaving by paying them more, and the Big 12 is trying to
keep its whole membership intact by paying everyone the same. The filings
cannot tell you motive. They can tell you the direction each conference moved,
and it was opposite.

The next Big 12 return, for the year ending June 30, 2026, is the first that
can show the flat policy.

## Section 5. The correction

Its own section, near the front if the correction title is chosen, here if
not. Either way it does not get buried.

In Part 1 I wrote that the Big Ten passes playoff money to the school that
earned it, and that this is what opened the gap inside the conference in
2024-25. Part 3 repeated it. That is wrong, or at least imprecise enough to
be misleading, and the filing I was already looking at says so.

The Big Ten's full-share payouts before 2024-25:

| Season | Schools | Spread |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2020-21 | 11 | $185,000 |
| 2021-22 | 11 | $175,000 |
| 2022-23 | 12 | $175,000 |
| 2023-24 | 12 | $375,001 |
| 2024-25 | 16 | $15,540,424 |

Four years inside $375,001, which is flatter than the ACC ever was, then a
$15,540,424 spread. I read that as the conference passing playoff money
through. Here is what the numbers actually support.

Median of the thirteen Big Ten full-share schools that played no playoff game:
$78,057,558. Against that:

| School | Playoff games | Premium | Per game |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Indiana | 1 | $2,952,339 | $2,952,339 |
| Penn State | 3 | $10,863,604 | $3,621,201 |
| Ohio State | 4 | $13,494,524 | $3,373,631 |

Indiana played one playoff game and received $2,952,339 more than the
non-playoff median. The playoff pays a $3,000,000 travel allowance per game.
That is a gap of $47,661.

A conference passing prize money through would have paid Indiana $4,000,000
for the bid on top of travel, and Ohio State far more than $13,494,524 for
winning the national championship, since the prize money for that run is
$20,000,000 before travel. It didn't. The Big Ten splits the prize money
equally and passes through the travel, which is what Forbes describes as its
long-standing policy.

What I can't explain: Penn State and Ohio State both received more than
$3,000,000 per game, $621,201 and $373,631 more respectively, and Penn State's
per-game figure is higher than Ohio State's despite the shorter run. Something
else is in those two numbers and the filing doesn't break it out. Say that
plainly rather than inventing a mechanism.

So the correction is specific: the Big Ten's 2024-25 gap is travel
reimbursement, not prize money. The gap is real and the size is right. The
cause I named was wrong.

## Section 6. Where the series lands

Four conferences, four policies, one playoff.

- **Big Ten.** Prize money split equally, travel passed through. Four years
  within $375,001, then a gap that is a travel line.
- **SEC.** Appears to pool. Fourteen full members within $4,116,525 in a year
  when three of them made the playoff.
- **ACC.** Passes postseason money through, including bowls and basketball, in
  its first year of trying. Spread went from four years inside $3,555,690 to
  $12,314,878.
- **Big 12.** Paid a participation formula for at least five years, then
  abolished it and went equal in 2025-26.

The same twelve-team bracket, the same money entering four conference offices,
and four different answers to who keeps it. What a school receives for a
playoff run depends less on the run than on which conference office the check
lands in first.

## Section 7. What would make this wrong

- The Big 12's formula is reported, not filed. Arizona State's $3,856,336
  premium is consistent with one game under the reported schedule and does not
  reproduce it exactly.
- The Big Ten correction rests on Indiana's single-game premium landing within
  $47,661 of the travel allowance. One school, one game. Penn State's and Ohio
  State's per-game figures do not land as cleanly, and if the $3,000,000 read
  is coincidence, the correction is wrong too.
- Conference fiscal years are not aligned, and none of these returns itemize
  postseason money as its own line.
- Arizona State's premium could include Big 12 championship game money, since
  it won the conference, and the filing does not separate that from playoff
  money.

## Section 8. What this predicts

- The Big 12 return for the year ending June 30, 2026 should show the flat
  policy: full-share schools inside roughly $2,000,000 of each other, with no
  school pulling clear on the strength of a playoff run, despite Texas Tech's
  2025 season. If a gap the size of Arizona State's appears again, the flat
  policy either didn't take effect or doesn't work the way it was reported.
- The Big Ten return for the same year should show a premium of about
  $3,000,000 per playoff game for its participants and no prize-money
  pass-through. If a Big Ten playoff team clears the median by far more than
  $3,000,000 a game, this correction needs correcting.

Same rule as always: both get checked here in public.

## Voice and standards

- No em dashes. Exact numbers, never rounded except announced figures.
  Contractions. US spelling. Month Day, Year. Serial commas.
- The correction is the piece's credibility. It does not get softened, and it
  does not get put at the end.
- Judgments labeled: the opposite-directions reading in section 4, and the
  characterization of the Big 12 as never having been equal-share. The
  spreads, premiums, and per-game figures are arithmetic.
- Policy descriptions are attributed to named reporting. Filing figures are
  attributed to the returns. Never blur the two.
