# Two conferences have a spending gap. Two conferences have one rich school.

*Alternate titles:*

*1. Every one of the eleven biggest rosters in college football is ranked. Below that, the money stops explaining anything.*

*2. The ACC has the widest spending range in the sport. Miami is the whole reason.*

*3. Houston is ranked 22nd on the 61st biggest roster budget in college football. (Tied for 61st with Oklahoma State on midpoint; see the tie note in section 3 before using this one.)*

---

*Construction note, and it's a different one this time. The two conference pieces ran on Form 990s, which are audited filings with a date and a signature. This one doesn't. The numbers below come from The Athletic's roster budget report on 68 teams, published September 16, 2026, by Stewart Mandel, David Ubben, Sam Khan Jr. and the Athletic Colleges Team. They built it from more than 70 sources: coaches, general managers, personnel directors, athletic directors, agents. At least one person at every school was contacted. The figures cover the money a program had to build its 2026 roster, which means athletic department revenue, revenue sharing, and booster or collective money combined, and they exclude income the program doesn't control, like a player's national endorsement deal. Every number The Athletic published is a range. Every midpoint in this piece is mine, calculated from the range, not reported. AP rankings are as of September 13, 2026. This is an estimate dataset, not an audited one, and everything that follows should be read at that confidence level.*

I want to quote The Athletic's own description of the bias in its sources rather than paraphrase it, because I can't remember a survey piece being this candid. Few people inside a program know the exact number and many who do won't share it, even anonymously. Coaches undersell their budgets, to manage fan expectations or to keep players from asking for more. Administrators oversell, to look like effective fundraisers. And programs routinely inflate what they believe rivals are spending, to argue internally for a bigger budget of their own. The Athletic says it weighted rival-sourced figures more skeptically as a result. So every figure here is the output of a correction The Athletic applied to a set of self-interested estimates, and the correction is theirs, not mine.

With that said, the report has a shape, and the shape is worth writing down.

## At the top, money works perfectly

The eleven largest roster budgets in the dataset all belong to AP-ranked teams. No exceptions.

| School | Budget | Midpoint | AP |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ohio State | $49-54M | $51.5M | 6 |
| Oregon | $48-54M | $51.0M | 21 |
| Texas | $45-55M | $50.0M | 1 |
| LSU | $47-50M | $48.5M | 7 |
| Texas A&M | $45-50M | $47.5M | 9 |
| Miami | $44-50M | $47.0M | 5 |
| Notre Dame | $41-48M | $44.5M | 3 |
| Ole Miss | $39-45M | $42.0M | 8 |
| Tennessee | $41-43M | $42.0M | 15 |
| Alabama | $38-42M | $40.0M | 10 |
| Texas Tech | $38-42M | $40.0M | 13 |

Eleven rather than ten because Alabama and Texas Tech tie at a $40.0M midpoint, same $38-42M range, and a cut at ten would mean breaking that tie by hand; eleven is where the data draws the line, not where I did.

Ranked teams average $35.5M on midpoint. Unranked teams average $22.4M. All 25 AP teams are among the 68 schools The Athletic covered, so that comparison isn't distorted by a ranked team missing from the sample.

I'm not going to claim causation from this. The correlation at the top is perfect in a single September snapshot, and the reason to lead with it is that it comes apart so fast once you look past the eleventh row.

## Where it stops working

Of the 25 largest budgets, 18 belong to ranked teams. Seven don't.

Five of those seven, Florida, South Carolina, Auburn, Nebraska, and Clemson, are programs that don't usually go unranked. That's a judgment about reputation, not a measurement, and you're free to disagree with it. The other two, Wisconsin and UCLA, are on the list because of the money alone.

| School | Budget | Midpoint | Budget rank |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Florida | $31-41M | $36.0M | 15 |
| South Carolina | $32-35M | $33.5M | 16 |
| Auburn | $31-35M | $33.0M | 17-18 |
| Nebraska | $31-35M | $33.0M | 17-18 |
| Clemson | $29-32M | $30.5M | 21-22 |
| Wisconsin | $27-31M | $29.0M | 23 |
| UCLA | $26-30M | $28.0M | 25 |

Where a rank shows as a range, the school's midpoint ties with another school's. Auburn and Nebraska tie with each other; Clemson ties with Penn State. I'd rather show the tie than invent an order.

Run the same sort the other direction and seven ranked teams sit outside the top 25 in spending.

| School | AP | Budget | Midpoint | Budget rank |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| BYU | 11 | $20-25M | $22.5M | 40-41 |
| SMU | 16 | $21-23M | $22.0M | 42-44 |
| Utah | 17 | $18-22M | $20.0M | 50-52 |
| Iowa | 18 | $17-21M | $19.0M | 55 |
| Houston | 22 | $15-19M | $17.0M | 61-62 |
| Louisville | 23 | $22-25M | $23.5M | 33-35 |
| Virginia | 25 | $20-24M | $22.0M | 42-44 |

Same rule on the ranges. BYU ties with North Carolina, SMU and Virginia tie with each other and with California, Utah ties with Minnesota and West Virginia, Louisville with Arkansas and TCU, and Houston with Oklahoma State. Iowa's $19.0M midpoint is its own, 55th of 68 with nobody beside it.

Houston is the extreme, and the tie it sits on is the sharpest single illustration in the dataset. Houston's $17.0M midpoint is shared with Oklahoma State, so the 22nd-ranked team in the country is 61st or 62nd of 68 in spending, with only six schools below it. The team it's tied with, Oklahoma State, beat Oregon on September 12. The Athletic makes that point itself in the September 16, 2026 report, noting that Oregon, believed to be spending around $50 million, lost to an Oklahoma State team estimated below $20 million. What the dataset adds is the tie: Oklahoma State isn't only a cheap team, it's tied for 61st of 68 with the 22nd-ranked team in the country, and Oregon's $51.0M midpoint, second largest in the sport, is three times Oklahoma State's.

Iowa deserves its own paragraph, because it's the one case on that list with a model attached to it. Kirk Ferentz builds from the offensive and defensive lines out and keeps players on campus rather than reloading through the portal, and 43 Iowa linemen have been drafted since 2000, per Brandon Marcello at CBS Sports on August 24, 2026. The Athletic's data shows that model still producing a ranked team on a budget 55th of 68. Whether it keeps doing so is a question for December, and I come back to it below.

So the top eleven is money. The next fourteen is something money doesn't explain, and the ranked teams below that are running on budgets that, on this data, shouldn't produce ranked teams.

## Two structures, one label

This is the part I think matters most, and it's also the part where I move from arithmetic into interpretation, so I'll mark the line when I cross it.

The Athletic describes the ACC as a league with little separation in roster cost. Measured by raw spread, the ACC is the widest conference in the country: Miami's $47.0M midpoint sits $36.5M above Boston College's $10.5M. The Big Ten's spread, Ohio State's $51.5M to Purdue's $18.0M, is $33.5M. The SEC's is $26.5M and the Big 12's is $25.0M.

Both descriptions are true, and the reason they can both be true is one school. Remove the single largest spender from each conference and the spreads separate.

| Conference | Full spread | Minus top spender | Who that is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ACC | $36.5M | $20.0M | Miami |
| Big 12 | $25.0M | $8.5M | Texas Tech |
| Big Ten | $33.5M | $33.0M | Ohio State |
| SEC | $26.5M | $25.0M | Texas |

Standard deviation, computed on midpoints, says the same thing. The ACC falls from $7.9M to $5.1M without Miami and the Big 12 from $6.0M to $2.8M without Texas Tech. The Big Ten moves from $10.4M to $9.0M without Ohio State, and the SEC from $8.7M to $8.0M without Texas.

One more cut, and this one uses a threshold I chose, so treat the number as a judgment about where to draw a line rather than something the data handed me. Count the schools in each conference with a midpoint above $30M. The SEC has 11 of 16. The Big Ten has 7 of 18. The ACC has 2 of 17, Miami and Clemson. The Big 12 has 1 of 16, Texas Tech.

Everything above this sentence is arithmetic on the CSV. What follows is what I think the arithmetic means.

These are two different competitive structures sharing one label. The ACC and the Big 12 are compressed fields with one rich outlier apiece; pull Miami and Texas Tech out and what's left in each league is a pack. The Big Ten and the SEC are stratified, a tier sitting on top of a tier, and pulling out the richest school changes almost nothing because the next school down is nearly as rich. A conference where one program is rich is not the same problem as a conference where eleven of sixteen clear a $30M line I drew, and the public conversation about spending gaps in college football hasn't been drawing that distinction. It should.

The Big Ten is the cleanest illustration. Ohio State at $49-54M and Oregon at $48-54M are a tier, not an outlier; there are two of them and they're within $0.5M of each other on midpoint. The drop from there to Purdue at $16-20M is the widest functional range in the sport, and it's a range with a whole conference distributed across it rather than one school and then everybody else.

## What would make this wrong

Same rule as the last two pieces. If I can't say what would break the argument, I haven't finished it.

The budgets are survey estimates. Other outlets polled other panels this year and produced different figures for some of the same programs, and if The Athletic's panel skewed in some direction, every ratio in this piece skews with it. I've used one source because it's the only one that covers all 68 schools with a stated method, not because I've verified its numbers against anything, since there's nothing to verify them against.

AP rank on September 13 is two weeks into a season and still mostly preseason expectation. It's closer to a measure of what people thought would happen than of what has happened, and that is the single largest weakness in this piece. A perfect top eleven in the budget table might just mean that the people who vote in the AP poll and the people who estimate budgets are working from the same priors about the same programs.

One season, one snapshot. Eleven for eleven at the top could be a coincidence of one year, and I have no second year to check it against.

Midpoints are mine, computed from published ranges, and a wide range hides more than a narrow one. Texas at $45-55M and Florida at $31-41M each carry $10M of uncertainty inside a single midpoint, where Tennessee at $41-43M carries $2M. Any ordering that depends on where Texas or Florida falls inside its range is an ordering I'd hold loosely.

Ties. Six of the seven cheap ranked teams sit on midpoints they share with at least one other school, and the top of the budget table ends on a tie between Alabama and Texas Tech, which is why the first section counts eleven instead of ten. None of the claims above turn on which way a tie breaks, and I've shown the ranges rather than picking, but a reader should know the ranks are softer than a single integer suggests.

Texas Tech's figure reportedly doesn't reflect roughly $3-4M freed up when quarterback Brendan Sorsby left the program in mid-2026, and Texas Tech is load-bearing for the Big 12 half of the two-structures argument. If its effective spend is lower than $38-42M, the Big 12's outlier is smaller than I've made it, though it's still the only school in the conference above $30M by a margin of $16.5M over TCU.

## What this predicts

Same convention as the SEC piece. If the reasoning holds, it should say something checkable before the check happens.

If money mostly buys the top and explains little below it, then the seven unranked big spenders, Florida, South Carolina, Auburn, Nebraska, Clemson, Wisconsin, and UCLA, shouldn't converge on the ranked group as the season goes on, and at least some of the seven cheap ranked teams, BYU, SMU, Utah, Iowa, Houston, Louisville, and Virginia, should still be ranked in the final AP poll of the regular season.

If instead the big spenders climb into the rankings and the cheap teams fall out by November, then the September snapshot was measuring preseason expectation rather than anything structural, and this piece was built on noise. I'd rather write that sentence now than have someone else write it in December.

The eleven-for-eleven top of the table is the softer prediction. I'd expect most of those eleven to still be ranked at the end of the regular season, but I wouldn't bet on all eleven, and if it's nine or ten the correlation still held about as well as a correlation on estimates can.

I'll revisit all of it after the final regular-season poll.

## The data

The dataset is 68 rows, one per school, with the conference, The Athletic's low and high estimates, the midpoint I calculated, and the September 13 AP rank where one exists: `cfb-roster-budgets-2026/data/cfb-roster-budgets-2026.csv` in the hqsports-research repository, with a README that carries the source, the retrieval method, and the caveats above.

Take it and check the work. This time the numbers underneath are estimates rather than filings, so check the arithmetic and then decide for yourself how much weight the estimates can carry.
