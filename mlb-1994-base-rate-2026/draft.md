# Baseball's first two strikes recovered in two years. The one over a salary cap took thirteen.

*The owners have proposed a cap for the first time since 1994. Here's what the attendance record says a stoppage costs.*

---

*Construction note, before anything else. Every attendance figure here comes from Baseball Reference's Major League year-by-year totals, retrieved September 24, 2026: one source, one series, 1970 through 2026.*

*Sources disagree on the headline numbers, so I'll say which one I'm using. ESPN's published table puts 1994 at 31,632 per game and 1995 at 25,260; Baseball Reference has 31,256 and 25,021, and other outlets quote one or the other. I took every figure from the Baseball Reference series so the comparisons run like for like. If you check against a different source you'll find small differences, and none of them change the conclusions.*

*Games lost in the 1994-95 strike get the same treatment. I use the AP's chronology, which counts 921: 669 regular-season games in 1994 and 252 in 1995. Other sources run from 931 to 948. The difference doesn't touch anything measured here, since every attendance figure is per game.*

*Baseball Reference excludes 2020, since no fans were admitted. Capacity limits held down the early part of 2021, and 2026 is still in progress. Everything is per game, never season totals, because the strike years and the expansion years played different numbers of games.*

---

## The base rate

Three MLB work stoppages have cost regular-season games, and two of them barely registered in the stands.

| Stoppage | Games lost | Drop from the year before | First year above the year before |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1972 strike | 86 | -3.7% in 1972 | 1973, two years after 1971 |
| 1981 strike | 712 | -6.8% in 1981 | 1982, two years after 1980 |
| 1994-95 strike | 921 | -19.2% in 1995 | 2006, thirteen years after 1993 |

The 1972 strike took per-game attendance from 15,063 in 1971 to 14,506. By 1973 it was 15,496, 2.9% above where it started. In 1981 the league went from 20,434 to 19,041 and came back in 1982 at 21,161, 3.6% over 1980. Both dips were gone a season after the strike.

1994 is where the pattern breaks, and it cost more than the other two strikes combined: 921 games by the AP's count, 669 in 1994 and 252 in 1995. The league drew 30,964 per game in 1993 and was running at 31,256 when the players walked out on August 12, 1994, a record pace. The strike ended April 2, 1995, the season was cut to 144 games, and attendance came in at 25,021. Then 26,509 in 1996, 27,876 in 1997, and every year after that below 1993 until 2006, when it reached 31,306.

That's thirteen years to clear the last full season before the strike. There's a second way to measure it, against the 1994 record pace of 31,256 instead of the full 1993 season, and that one comes out at twelve years, also to 2006. The margin in 2006 was thin by either measure: 342 per game above 1993, 50 above the 1994 pace.

So the base rate for a baseball stoppage is a two-year dip. 1994 is the exception, thirteen years against two.

## What made 1994 different

This section is a judgment. Three events can't isolate a cause, and I won't pretend they can. What I can do is list what 1994 had that 1972 and 1981 didn't.

**It canceled the World Series,** the first time the sport had gone without one since 1904. Neither earlier strike reached the postseason.

**It ran 232 days,** across two seasons, longer than either of the others.

**It ended by injunction.** The players went back to work with no new collective bargaining agreement in place, so nothing was actually settled when the games resumed.

**It was about a salary cap.** The 1972 strike was about pensions. A fight over the structure of the sport is harder to close than a fight over a benefit, and 1994's didn't close so much as stop.

My read is that the canceled World Series and the lack of a settlement did the most damage, because they told fans the sport couldn't finish a season or fix what broke it. That's a reading of three data points, and I'm labeling it as one.

## The parallel to December 1

The collective bargaining agreement expires at 11:59 p.m. ET on December 1, 2026, and the owners have proposed a salary cap for the first time since 1994. According to reporting by CBS Sports, ESPN, and Sportico, the proposal includes:

- A cap of $245.3 million and a floor of $171.2 million for 2027, benefits included
- Free agents who change teams limited to five-year contracts at 15% of payroll
- A minimum salary raised from $780,000 to $1 million

The union's position is that a cap isn't negotiable. ESPN describes the gap as one of frameworks: the two sides aren't haggling over numbers inside a system both accept. That's the kind of fight 1994 was.

It isn't the kind 2021-22 was. That lockout ran 99 days and cost no regular-season games, and 2022 finished at 26,566 per game, 5.8% below 2019. It would be convenient to call that the cost of a short lockout, and I can't, because 2022 was also the first full-capacity season after the pandemic. The two effects can't be pulled apart in this data, so the 2022 number doesn't count for or against anything in this piece.

## Where baseball stands going in

Most 1994 comparisons stop at the recovery. The part they skip is what happened after it.

Per-game attendance passed the 1994 pace in 2006 and peaked the next year at 32,696. It has cleared 31,256 in exactly one season since, 2008, at 32,382. In 2026 the league is drawing 29,460 per game, which is 5.7% below the 1994 pre-strike pace and 9.9% below the 2007 peak.

The last cap fight took twelve years to recover from, measured against the pace it interrupted, and the sport is now below that pace again. December 1, 2026 comes nineteen years after the high-water mark. A stoppage in 2027 wouldn't be interrupting a boom.

Any reason I give for the slide after 2007 is a judgment, because the decline has more than one plausible cause. The next section names the ones this piece can't sort out.

## What would make this wrong

Three events is not a base rate in any statistical sense. It's the entire history of baseball stoppages that cost games, which is why it's worth using, but it's still three, and one of them is the outlier the whole argument rests on.

New ballparks confound the 1994-2006 recovery. A wave of new stadiums opened in those years, and new stadiums raise attendance on their own. Some of what I've credited to time passing belongs to construction, and I haven't measured how much.

The 1993 baseline carries its own lift. The league went from 26 teams to 28 that year, and per-game attendance jumped 16.7%, from 26,529 to 30,964. If some of that was expansion novelty that would have faded anyway, the thirteen-year measure overstates the strike's damage. The twelve-year measure, against the 1994 pace, has the same problem, since 1994 was the second season with 28 teams.

Ticket prices confound the post-2007 decline. Attendance fell over the same years prices rose, and this piece can't separate the two. The next one will try.

Attendance isn't the main revenue line anymore. Media rights matter far more to team finances now than they did in 1994, and a fan who stops going to games but keeps watching them costs a team less than that fan used to. A 2027 attendance drop the size of 1995's would hurt, but it wouldn't hurt the owners the way 1995 did.

Expansion changes the denominator. The league went from 28 teams to 30 in 1998. Per-game figures handle that better than totals, but not perfectly.

The 2021-22 lockout tells us nothing, for the pandemic reasons above.

## What this predicts

If 1994 is the right comparison, the variable that matters is whether games are lost, and above all whether the postseason is.

A lockout that ends before Opening Day has no clean precedent in this series. The only one is 2022, and the pandemic ruins it as a test, so the honest prediction is that a short lockout shouldn't register much and I can't prove it from here. A lockout that costs regular-season games should dip the way 1972 and 1981 did, 3.7% and 6.8%, and come back within two years. A lockout that reaches the 2027 postseason should look like 1995: 2027 per-game attendance falling by something closer to 19.2% than to 3.7%.

All of that is checkable against Baseball Reference's 2027 totals, and I'll check it here when they post.
