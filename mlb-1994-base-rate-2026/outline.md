# Article outline: the 1994 strike as the base rate for a work stoppage

First baseball piece on Ledger & Whistle, pulled forward from the November
lockout series. The collective bargaining agreement expires at 11:59 p.m. ET on
December 1, 2026, and owners have proposed a salary cap for the first time since
1994. This piece asks what the historical record says a stoppage costs, measured
rather than asserted.

Ticket prices are named here as a confound this piece cannot resolve. They get
their own piece next, built on twenty-five years of Wrigley Field invoices.

## Working titles

1. Baseball's last two strikes recovered in two years. The one over a salary
   cap took thirteen.
2. The owners want a salary cap. The last time they tried, attendance didn't
   recover until 2006.
3. Baseball never fully recovered from 1994. It's about to find out whether it
   can afford another one.

Number 1 is the finding. Number 3 is the sharpest but leans on the post-2007
decline, which has more than one cause. Label accordingly if chosen.

## Section 1. Construction note

- Attendance is from Baseball Reference's Major League year-by-year totals,
  retrieved September 24, 2026. One source, one series, 1970 through 2026.
- **Sources disagree on the headline numbers, and that's worth saying.** ESPN's
  published table puts 1994 at 31,632 per game and 1995 at 25,260. Baseball
  Reference has 31,256 and 25,021. Other outlets quote either. Every figure in
  this piece comes from the single Baseball Reference series so the comparisons
  are like for like. Anyone checking against a different source will find
  small differences, and the conclusions don't change.
- 2020 is excluded by the source, since fans weren't admitted. 2021 was limited
  by capacity restrictions early in the season. 2026 is a season in progress.
- Per-game figures are used throughout rather than totals, because the three
  strike seasons and the expansion years played different numbers of games.

## Section 2. The base rate

Three MLB work stoppages have cost regular-season games. Two of them barely
registered.

| Stoppage | Games lost | Drop in the stoppage year | Recovered above the pre-stoppage year |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1972 strike | 86 | -3.7% | 1973, two years later |
| 1981 strike | 712 | -6.8% | 1982, two years later |
| 1994-95 strike | 921 | -19.2% in 1995 | 2006, thirteen years later |

Pre-stoppage years and exact figures:

- 1971: 15,063 per game. 1972: 14,506. 1973: 15,496, 2.9% above 1971.
- 1980: 20,434. 1981: 19,041. 1982: 21,161, 3.6% above 1980.
- 1993: 30,964. 1994: 31,256 at the strike, a record pace. 1995: 25,021.
  1996: 26,509. 1997: 27,876. Not above 1993 until 2006, at 31,306.

Measured against the 1994 record pace of 31,256 rather than the full 1993
season, recovery took twelve years, to 2006.

The finding: the base rate for a baseball stoppage is a two-year dip. 1994 is
the exception, and it's the exception by a factor of six.

## Section 3. What made 1994 different

Label this section a judgment. Three events are not enough to isolate a cause,
and the piece shouldn't pretend otherwise. What it can do is name what 1994 had
that 1972 and 1981 didn't.

- **It canceled the World Series,** the first time since 1904. Neither earlier
  stoppage touched the postseason.
- **It was the longest,** 232 days, spanning two seasons.
- **It ended by injunction, not agreement.** Players returned without a new
  collective bargaining agreement in place.
- **It was about a salary cap.** 1972 was about pensions. 1994 was a fight over
  the structure of the sport, which is harder to settle and was not settled.

## Section 4. The parallel to December 1

Owners have proposed a salary cap for the first time since 1994. As reported by
CBS Sports, ESPN, and Sportico:

- A cap of $245.3 million and a floor of $171.2 million for 2027, including
  benefits
- Free agents changing teams limited to five-year contracts at 15% of payroll
- The minimum salary raised from $780,000 to $1 million

The union's position is that a cap is not negotiable. ESPN describes the gap as
different frameworks rather than different numbers inside an agreed system,
which is the same kind of fight 1994 was and not the kind 2021-22 was.

The 2021-22 lockout ran 99 days and cost no regular-season games. 2022 came in
at 26,566 per game, 5.8% below 2019. That's confounded by the pandemic, so it
can't carry weight either way. State that plainly rather than using it.

## Section 5. Where baseball stands going in

The part the 1994 comparisons usually skip.

- Per-game attendance recovered past the 1994 mark in 2006 and peaked in 2007
  at 32,696.
- 2026 is running at 29,460 per game.
- That's 5.7% below the 1994 pre-strike pace and 9.9% below the 2007 peak.

So the recovery from the last cap fight took twelve years, and the sport is now
below where it started. A stoppage in 2027 wouldn't be interrupting a boom. It
would arrive nineteen years after the high-water mark.

**Label the causal part a judgment.** The post-2007 decline has more than one
explanation, and the next section names them.

## Section 6. What would make this wrong

- **Three events is not a base rate in any statistical sense.** It's the whole
  history of stoppages that cost games, which is the point, but it's still
  three.
- **New ballparks confound the 1994-2006 recovery.** A wave of new stadiums
  opened in those years and raised attendance on their own. Some of the
  recovery credited to time passing belongs to construction.
- **Ticket prices confound the post-2007 decline.** Attendance fell over the
  same years prices rose. This piece can't separate those, and the next one
  will try.
- **Attendance is no longer the main revenue line.** Media rights matter far
  more to team finances now than in 1994. A fan who stops attending but keeps
  watching costs a team less than it used to.
- **Expansion changes the denominator.** The league went from 28 to 30 teams in
  1998. Per-game figures handle this better than totals, but not perfectly.
- **The 2021-22 lockout is uninformative** because of the pandemic.

## Section 7. What this predicts

If 1994 is the right comparison, the variable that matters is whether games are
lost, and especially whether the postseason is. A lockout that ends before
Opening Day should look like 2022 and not show up in attendance. A lockout that
costs regular-season games should dip. A lockout that reaches the 2027
postseason should look like 1995, with 2027 per-game attendance falling by
something closer to 19% than 5%.

Checkable against Baseball Reference's 2027 totals.
