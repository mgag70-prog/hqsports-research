Baseball's owners want a salary cap, and part of their case is that a cap makes franchises worth more. Only one league has adopted a cap inside Forbes' valuation record, the NHL in 2005. For the seven years after it, baseball teams gained value faster than hockey teams did.

## Post 2 (248/280)

The proposal, per the AP on May 28, 2026: a $245.3 million cap for 2027, a $171.2 million floor, players at 50% of revenue, and local media money pooled and split equally in place of the clubs' current revenue-sharing plan, under a seven-year deal.

## Post 3 (274/280)

Attach: `figures/fig-02-ratio.png`

On Forbes' 2004 lists, the last before hockey's cap, the average MLB team was worth 1.81 times the average NHL team. On the 2011 lists, seven years in, 2.18 times. The gap widened under the cap. It peaked at 2.83 in 2020 and was 1.18 by 2025, the years of hockey's TV deals.

## Post 4 (277/280)

Attach: `figures/fig-03-growth.png`

Growth in average team value, a year. Before the cap, 1999 to 2004 lists: hockey 3.84%, baseball 6.02%. After it, 2004 to 2011: hockey 5.68%, baseball 8.52%. Both grew faster after 2004; the gap in annual growth went from 2.18 points to 2.84. Forbes estimates, not sale prices.

## Post 5 (271/280)

Attach: `figures/fig-04-multiples.png`

Forbes had baseball at 2.5 times revenue on its March 2011 list and hockey at 2.5 on its 2012 list: seven years into the cap, the same multiple. As cited on March 20, 2026: MLB 7.0, NHL 9, NFL 10.7, NBA 12.9. Baseball already pools 48% of net local revenue between clubs.
