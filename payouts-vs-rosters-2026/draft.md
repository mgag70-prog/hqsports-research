# Miami and Duke got conference checks $14,349 apart. Miami's roster costs three times Duke's.

*The conference check is the one thing that's nearly equal, and it isn't what separates these programs.*

---

*Construction note, and a longer one than usual, because this is the first piece here that puts two datasets of different quality in the same table. Read this before any number below, because every number below depends on it.*

*The conference distributions are audited Form 990 filings for fiscal years ending in 2025, retrieved through ProPublica. They're the same figures behind [Part 1](https://www.gridironhq.ai/blog/college-football-playoff-conference-revenue-sharing), [Part 2](https://www.gridironhq.ai/blog/sec-playoff-distribution-revenue-sharing), and [Part 3](https://ledgerandwhistle.substack.com/p/the-acc-paid-its-schools-within-36) of the distributions series. When I write that Duke received $48,236,296 from the ACC, that's a line on a tax return the conference signed.*

*The roster budgets are estimates. The Athletic published them on September 16, 2026, built from more than 70 sources (coaches, general managers, personnel directors, athletic directors, and agents), with its own stated bias corrections: coaches undersell, administrators oversell, and rivals inflate what they think each other spends. Every figure The Athletic published is a range. Every midpoint here is mine, calculated as the middle of that range, and the midpoint is what the correlations and ratios run on. When I write that Duke's roster costs $12-15 million, that's a triangulated estimate from people who mostly won't say the number out loud.*  I wrote about those estimates when they came out, in [a piece on all 68 budgets against the rankings](https://ledgerandwhistle.substack.com/p/two-conferences-have-a-spending-gap).

*The two datasets don't cover the same period. The payouts are money received for the year ending in 2025. The budgets are for building the 2026 roster. Revenue sharing began in July 2025, between the two, and nothing here adjusts for it.*

*They don't measure the same thing either, and this is the caveat most likely to be misread. A conference distribution funds an entire athletic department: every sport, the facilities debt, the staff, the travel. A roster budget is football players only. Any ratio between them is a comparison of two different quantities, useful for showing relative scale and nothing more. It isn't a share of anything. I'll say this again at the ratio table, because that's where it'll get misread.*

*Only schools on full conference shares are compared. Partial, transition, buy-in, and media-forgone schools are excluded from every spread, correlation, and ratio, and they're listed by name in the section on schools that can't be compared, with the reason each one is out.*

---

## Same check, different roster

Miami received $48,250,645 from the ACC for the fiscal year ending June 30, 2025. Duke received $48,236,296. The gap is $14,349 on checks of a little over $48 million, and only one pair of full-share schools in any of the four conferences sits closer than that.

Miami's estimated roster budget for 2026 is $44-50 million. Duke's is $12-15 million. On midpoints, $47.0 million against $13.5 million, which is 3.48 times.

That's the whole argument in two numbers, and it isn't a one-off. Here are three more pairs from three other conferences, each on the same contract, with checks that are nearly the same and rosters that aren't.

| Pair | Conference | Payouts | Gap | Roster budgets |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arkansas and LSU | SEC | $73,083,520 and $72,391,720 | $691,800 | $22-25M and $47-50M |
| Texas Tech and Colorado | Big 12 | $39,734,106 and $39,034,422 | $699,684 | $38-42M and $18-21M |
| USC and Purdue | Big Ten | $78,057,558 and $77,732,782 | $324,776 | $37-40M and $16-20M |

Same conference, same media deal, same check within what any of these departments would call a rounding error, and rosters that differ by a factor of two or three. LSU's midpoint is 2.06 times Arkansas's, Texas Tech's is 2.05 times Colorado's, and USC's is 2.14 times Purdue's.

One note for the reader hunting for the tightest pair rather than the widest gap: Ole Miss and Arkansas are $7,035 apart, at $73,076,485 and $73,083,520, on rosters of $39-45 million and $22-25 million. Miami and Duke are the second-closest checks in the data and the widest roster gap among any pair of near-identical checks, which is why they lead.

## It holds across all four conferences

Within every conference, roster spending varies more than the money the conference hands out. The table is full-share schools only; spread means the highest figure minus the lowest, and the roster spread is on midpoints.

| Conference | Full-share schools | Payout spread | Roster spread (midpoint) | Ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SEC | 14 | $4,116,525 | $25,000,000 | 6.07x |
| Big 12 | 12 | $5,129,685 | $25,000,000 | 4.87x |
| ACC | 14 | $12,314,878 | $36,500,000 | 2.96x |
| Big Ten | 16 | $15,540,424 | $33,500,000 | 2.16x |

The SEC is the sharpest case. Fourteen schools sit within $4,116,525 of each other on the money in, from Georgia's $74,458,940 down to Mississippi State's $70,342,415, and their roster midpoints span $25,000,000, from LSU's $48.5 million down to Arkansas's $23.5 million. The payout spread is a rounding error on the roster spread.

The ordering of that table isn't an accident, and it connects to [Part 3](https://ledgerandwhistle.substack.com/p/the-acc-paid-its-schools-within-36) documented. The two conferences with the narrowest payout spreads, the SEC and the Big 12, are the two that distribute most equally. The ACC's and the Big Ten's spreads are wider because both pay schools for postseason success, which is the finding Part 3 documented in the ACC's filing. Even at their widest, though, the payout spread is a fraction of the roster spread. The Big Ten's $15,540,424 gap between Ohio State and UCLA is the largest in any conference, and it's still less than half the Big Ten's roster spread.

## Inside a conference, the check doesn't predict the roster

Spearman rank correlation between conference payout and roster budget midpoint, with tied values assigned their average rank, full-share schools only, same method as the same method as [the Big Ten piece](https://ledgerandwhistle.substack.com/p/in-the-big-ten-money-explains-the).

| Conference | n | Correlation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Big Ten | 16 | 0.437 |
| Big 12 | 12 | 0.357 |
| SEC | 14 | 0.288 |
| ACC | 14 | 0.169 |

Across all 56 full-share schools the correlation is 0.465, and that number misleads on its own. It mostly reflects which conference a school is in, not where it sits inside one. Median payouts run from $78,781,120 in the Big Ten and $72,767,380 in the SEC down to $46,817,210 in the ACC and $39,427,303.50 in the Big 12. Median roster midpoints follow the same split, though not the same order: $33.25 million in the SEC and $25.5 million in the Big Ten, then $23.0 million in the ACC and $20.0 million in the Big 12. The two conferences that pay the most have the two most expensive rosters, and the two that pay the least have the two cheapest. That between-conference structure is most of the 0.465. Inside any single conference the relationship is much weaker, and in the ACC it's close to nothing.

Now the conclusion, which is a judgment and not a measurement. The conference check tells you which league a school plays in. It doesn't tell you what that school will spend on players. Something else does, and whatever it is, it isn't on any of these filings.

## The schools that can't be compared

Same discipline as [Part 1](https://www.gridironhq.ai/blog/college-football-playoff-conference-revenue-sharing)'s asterisked schools. Twelve of the 68 programs in The Athletic's report are on something other than a full conference share for the fiscal year in the filing, and none of them belong in a spread, a correlation, or a ratio. Listing them is the point: a reader who goes looking for Texas in the tables above should find the reason it isn't there.

Texas received $12,113,287 and Oklahoma received $2,575,481 in their first full SEC year on transition terms, with no media rights distribution under their entry agreement, against estimated rosters of $45-55 million and $28-34 million. On the raw numbers Texas's roster is 4.128 times its check and Oklahoma's is 12.037 times, and both figures are meaningless, because the check is a transition payment and not a share.

SMU took $17,065,303 from the ACC, having agreed to forgo all ACC media revenue during its early membership. Notre Dame took $18,142,243 as an ACC member outside football, and its NBC football money is paid directly to the school and never appears in a conference filing.

California and Stanford are on reduced ACC entry shares, 33% of media rights under their entry agreement, at $22,993,712 and $19,557,463. Oregon and Washington are on partial Big Ten shares through 2030 under their entry agreement, at $48,416,125 and $46,708,168. BYU, Cincinnati, Houston, and UCF are on partial Big 12 entry shares, at $23,110,622, $20,211,539, $19,881,951, and $19,978,520, with full shares beginning in 2025-26.

That leaves 56 schools on full shares: 16 in the Big Ten, 14 in the SEC, 14 in the ACC, and 12 in the Big 12. Everything above runs on those 56.

## What this says about the donor-base argument

On September 21, 2026, Adam Niedbalski published ["College Football Has a Payroll Cap Now. It's Just Called Your Donor Base."](https://adamniedbalski.substack.com/p/college-football-has-a-payroll-cap) His argument, built on the same Athletic estimates, is that college football has become a payroll league: the old advantages all convert to capital, capital converts to roster, and the programs with the deepest financial ecosystems have the widest margin for error. His headline says the cap is the donor base. He didn't test that against the conference filings, and as far as I can find, nobody else did either.

The filings support the headline more than the argument.

Start with the headline. If the institutional money is nearly equal inside a conference and the rosters aren't, then the difference between Miami's roster and Duke's is coming from somewhere the conference doesn't control: collectives, donors, local sponsorship, and whatever else sits outside a conference 990. That's the donor base. It isn't disclosed anywhere, but it's measurable by subtraction, and the subtraction is large. This is a judgment, not an accounting identity: I'm inferring the source of the gap from what the filings rule out, not from anything that names it.

Niedbalski's own opening pair, Ohio State at $49-54 million against Boston College at $8-13 million, is a cross-conference comparison, and that's where the argument gets muddier. Ohio State's Big Ten check was $91,552,082 and Boston College's ACC check was $47,087,682, a gap of $44,464,400 before a single donor writes anything. Some of his payroll gap is conference money. The within-conference pairs above are the cleaner test of his headline, because they hold the conference check constant, and they come out the way he'd expect.

Where this publication disagreed with him earlier stays worth one line. He argues payroll now decides outcomes. In the Big Ten's middle fourteen, payroll and results have a Spearman correlation of -0.071 over 2024 and 2025. Money separates the programs. It doesn't, in the middle of a conference, separate the outcomes.

## What would make this wrong

The budgets are estimates and the payouts are filings, and they aren't the same kind of evidence. Every spread, correlation, and ratio here puts an audited figure on one axis and a triangulated range on the other. If The Athletic's panel skewed, whether because coaches undersold, administrators oversold, or the rival-reported figures got weighted wrong despite the correction, every roster spread and every ratio here skews with it. The payout side of each table would survive; the roster side wouldn't.

The two datasets cover different periods, one year apart, with the start of revenue sharing in July 2025 sitting between them. A school that received its check for the year ending in 2025 was building its 2026 roster under rules that didn't exist when the check was earned. The comparison is between the last year of the old money and the first year of the new spend.

A conference distribution funds a whole department. A school carrying heavy facilities debt, or a large portfolio of non-revenue sports, has less of that check left for football than the ratio implies, and nothing here adjusts for it. Two schools with identical checks and identical roster budgets can be in very different financial positions, and this piece would show them as identical.

Fiscal years aren't aligned across conferences. The SEC closes its books on August 31 and the other three close on June 30, so the SEC's filed year runs two months later than everyone else's. The within-conference comparisons don't care. The cross-conference medians do, a little.

One year of each. A school in the middle of a capital project or a coaching transition can look very different twelve months later on either axis, and this piece has no second year of roster estimates to check against.

The Big 12's spread ratio and correlation are computed on 12 schools, the fewest of the four. A rank correlation on 12 moves more with a single reordering than one on 16 does.

## What this predicts

If the check doesn't drive the roster, then next year's filings should show the payout spreads staying roughly where they are while the roster budgets keep diverging. That's checkable.

Specifically: the SEC's full-share payout spread should stay in single-digit millions once Texas and Oklahoma reach full shares and join the comparison, and its roster spread on midpoints should stay at or above $25,000,000. The ACC's and the Big Ten's payout spreads may widen again if their success payments keep growing, but they should stay well under their roster spreads. The four within-conference correlations should stay below 0.5, and the ACC's should stay the lowest of the four.

If instead the two spreads converge, with payout spreads widening toward the roster spreads or roster spreads collapsing toward the payout spreads, then conference money is doing more work than this piece credits, and the donor-base reading of the gap was wrong.

The Texas and Oklahoma case is the sharpest test. Their transition payments of $12,113,287 and $2,575,481 kept them out of this year's comparison. Next year they'll be full-share SEC schools with rosters estimated at $45-55 million and $28-34 million. If the SEC's payout spread stays within a few million of this year's $4,116,525 with those two inside it while its roster spread holds or grows, the argument holds in the conference where it's most exposed.

## The data

Two files, and they aren't the same kind of evidence.

The conference distributions are the same dataset behind Parts 1, 2, and 3, unchanged: one row per school-season, all five conferences, from audited Form 990 filings. [Download the CSV](https://www.gridironhq.ai/data/cfb-conference-distributions.csv). The 2024-25 rows are what this piece runs on.

The roster budgets are The Athletic's estimated ranges for 68 teams, published September 16, 2026, transcribed with the midpoint calculated here: `cfb-roster-budgets-2026/data/cfb-roster-budgets-2026.csv` in the hqsports-research repository, with a README that carries the source, the retrieval method, and the caveats. A third file in this piece's data folder, `payouts-vs-rosters-2026/data/payouts-vs-rosters-2026.csv`, joins the two: school, conference, share type, the 2024-25 payout, The Athletic's low and high estimates, the midpoint, the ratio, and the filing's note on any school not on a full share.

Every pair gap, spread, correlation, median, and ratio above was recomputed from those files before writing. The correlations are Spearman rank correlations with tied values assigned their average rank, the same rule as the Big Ten piece; a different tie rule moves the figures at the second decimal and not the conclusion. If you find a filed number that's wrong, tell me and I'll correct it, the way the Cal and Stanford share notes in the distributions file were corrected after WRAL corrected its own reporting. If you find an estimated number that's wrong, tell The Athletic, and then tell me.
