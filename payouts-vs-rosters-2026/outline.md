# Article outline: conference payouts against roster budgets

The fourth piece off this pair of datasets, and the first where filing-based
numbers and estimate-based numbers sit in the same table. That is the piece's
main methodological risk and it gets handled in the first section, not the
last.

It also answers a question someone else asked in public. On September 21,
2026, Adam Niedbalski published "College Football Has a Payroll Cap Now. It's
Just Called Your Donor Base," arguing that college football has become a
payroll league. His headline is a testable claim and nobody tested it,
including him. This piece tests it with the conference filings.

## Working titles

1. Miami and Duke got conference checks $14,349 apart. Miami's roster costs
   three times Duke's.
2. The conference check is the one thing that is nearly equal. It is not what
   separates these programs.
3. Texas Tech spends more on its roster than its conference pays it.

Number 1 is the hook and the cleanest pair in the data. Number 2 is the
finding.

---

## Section 1. Construction note, up top, and longer than usual

Two datasets of different quality are being joined, so say exactly what each
one is before any number appears.

- **Conference distributions** are audited Form 990 filings, fiscal years
  ending in 2025, retrieved through ProPublica. These are the same figures
  behind Parts 1, 2, and 3 of the distributions series.
- **Roster budgets** are estimates published by The Athletic on September 16,
  2026, built from more than 70 sources, with stated bias corrections. Every
  figure is a range; midpoints are calculated here.
- **They do not cover the same period.** The payouts are money received for
  the year ending in 2025. The budgets are for building the 2026 roster.
  Revenue sharing began in July 2025, between the two.
- **They do not measure the same thing.** A conference distribution funds an
  entire athletic department: every sport, facilities debt, staff, travel. A
  roster budget is football players only. Any ratio between them is a
  comparison of two different quantities, useful for showing relative scale
  and nothing more. This is the caveat most likely to be misread, so state it
  in those words and repeat it at the ratio table.
- Only schools on full conference shares are compared. Partial, transition,
  buy-in, and media-forgone schools are excluded and listed in section 6.

## Section 2. Same check, different roster

Open on the pair, because it does the whole argument in two numbers.

Miami received $48,250,645 from the ACC. Duke received $48,236,296. That is a
gap of $14,349 on checks of roughly $48 million, which is as close to
identical as two schools get in this data.

Miami's estimated roster budget is $44-50 million. Duke's is $12-15 million.

Three more pairs with nearly identical payouts and very different rosters:

| Pair | Payouts | Roster budgets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arkansas and LSU | $73,083,520 and $72,391,720 | $22-25M and $47-50M |
| Texas Tech and Colorado | $39,734,106 and $39,034,422 | $38-42M and $18-21M |
| USC and Purdue | $78,057,558 and $77,732,782 | $37-40M and $16-20M |

Same conference, same contract, same check within a rounding error, and
rosters that differ by two or three times.

## Section 3. It holds across all four conferences

Not anecdote. Within every conference, roster spending varies far more than
the money the conference distributes.

| Conference | Payout spread | Roster spread | Ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SEC | $4.1M | $25.0M | 6.1x |
| Big 12 | $5.1M | $25.0M | 4.9x |
| ACC | $12.3M | $36.5M | 3.0x |
| Big Ten | $15.5M | $33.5M | 2.2x |

The SEC is the sharpest case: fourteen schools within $4,116,525 of each other
on the money in, and a $25,000,000 range on the roster spend.

Note the ordering, because it is the connective tissue to Part 3. The two
conferences with the narrowest payout spreads are the two that distribute most
equally. The ACC's and the Big Ten's spreads are wider precisely because they
pay for postseason success, which Part 3 covered. Even at their widest, the
payout spread is a fraction of the roster spread.

## Section 4. Inside a conference, the check does not predict the roster

Rank correlation between conference payout and roster budget midpoint,
full-share schools only:

| Conference | n | Correlation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Big Ten | 16 | 0.437 |
| Big 12 | 12 | 0.357 |
| SEC | 14 | 0.288 |
| ACC | 14 | 0.169 |

Across all 56 full-share schools the correlation is 0.465, and that number is
misleading on its own: it mostly reflects which conference a school is in, not
where it sits inside one. Median payouts run from $78,781,120 in the Big Ten
down to $39,427,303 in the Big 12, and median roster budgets track that
ordering. Inside any single conference the relationship is much weaker.

**Label this a judgment.** The conference check tells you which league a
school plays in. It does not tell you what that school will spend on players.
Something else does, and it is not in any of these filings.

## Section 5. The ratio, and what it does not mean

Roster budget as a percentage of conference distribution. Restate here that
these are two different quantities and the percentage is a scale comparison,
not a share of a budget.

Highest:

| School | Roster | Payout | Ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Texas Tech | $38-42M | $39,734,106 | 101% |
| Miami | $44-50M | $48,250,645 | 97% |
| LSU | $47-50M | $72,391,720 | 67% |
| Texas A&M | $45-50M | $72,899,870 | 65% |

Lowest:

| School | Roster | Payout | Ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Boston College | $8-13M | $47,087,682 | 22% |
| Purdue | $16-20M | $77,732,782 | 23% |
| Iowa | $17-21M | $79,094,180 | 24% |
| Minnesota | $18-22M | $79,203,559 | 25% |

Texas Tech's estimated roster costs slightly more than everything the Big 12
paid it. Boston College's costs about a fifth of what the ACC paid it.

The Illinois and Iowa connection to the last piece is worth one line: both sit
in the bottom group here, and both finished in the top seven of the Big Ten
over two years. Cheap rosters, and in those two cases, not cheap results.

## Section 6. The schools that cannot be compared

Short, same discipline as Part 1's asterisked schools.

Texas received $12,113,287 and Oklahoma $2,575,481 in their first SEC year on
transition terms, against estimated rosters of $45-55M and $28-34M. SMU took
$17,065,303 having forgone media revenue. Notre Dame took $18,142,243 as a
member outside football, and its NBC money never appears in a conference
filing. California, Stanford, Oregon, and Washington are on reduced entry
shares; BYU, Cincinnati, Houston, and UCF on partial Big 12 shares.

None of these belong in the comparison. Listing them is the point: a reader
who goes looking for Texas in the table should find the reason it is not
there.

## Section 7. What this says about the donor-base argument

Engage Adam Niedbalski's piece directly and by name, linking it. He argued
college football is now a payroll league and titled it around the donor base.

The filings support the headline more than the argument. If the institutional
money is nearly equal inside a conference and the rosters are not, then the
difference is coming from somewhere the conference does not control:
collectives, donors, local sponsorship, and whatever else sits outside a
conference 990. That is the donor base, and it is measurable by subtraction
even though it is not disclosed anywhere.

Where the earlier piece in this publication disagreed with him stays worth
restating in one line: in the Big Ten's middle fourteen, payroll and results
have a correlation of -0.071. Money separates the programs. It does not, in
the middle of a conference, separate the outcomes.

## Section 8. What would make this wrong

- The budgets are estimates and the payouts are filings. If The Athletic's
  panel skewed, every ratio here skews with it.
- The two datasets cover different periods, one year apart, spanning the start
  of revenue sharing.
- A conference distribution funds a whole department. A school with heavy
  facilities debt or a large non-revenue sport portfolio has less left for
  football than the ratio implies, and nothing here adjusts for that.
- Fiscal years are not aligned across conferences. The SEC closes August 31,
  the others June 30.
- One year of each. A school in the middle of a capital project or a coaching
  transition can look very different twelve months later.

## Section 9. What this predicts

If the check does not drive the roster, then next year's filings should show
the payout spreads staying roughly where they are while roster budgets keep
diverging. Specifically: the SEC's payout spread should stay in single-digit
millions once Texas and Oklahoma reach full shares, while its roster spread
should stay at or above $25,000,000. If instead the two spreads converge,
conference money is doing more work than this piece credits.

Both files, both linked, with the difference in their evidentiary quality

## Voice and standards

- No em dashes. Exact numbers, never rounded except when quoting an announced
  figure. Contractions. US spelling. Month Day, Year. Serial commas.
- Judgments labeled. Section 4's conclusion and section 7's subtraction
  argument are judgments. The payouts, budgets, spreads, correlations, and
  ratios are arithmetic.
- The filing-versus-estimate distinction is the spine of the piece's
  credibility. It appears in section 1, again at the ratio table in section 5,
