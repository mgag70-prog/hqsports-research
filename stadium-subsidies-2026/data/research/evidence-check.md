# Stadium subsidy evidence check: 12 claims from the reader essay

Most of the core evidence holds up. Several numbers in the reader essay are slightly wrong or attributed to the wrong source.

- **Wrong:** the Propheter per-venue figure and the Cobb County range.
- **Paraphrased:** the Matheson quote.
- **Methodologically weak:** the FinanceBuzz ticket figures.
- **Second-hand:** Fortune's $33B and $13B figures. Cite the original sources instead.

---

## 1. Fortune, June 2026 — **Partly verified**

- **Article:** "American taxpayers have spent $33 billion on sports stadiums. They got fewer seats—and higher prices." By Catherina Gioino, June 11, 2026. https://fortune.com/2026/06/11/american-taxpayers-spent-33-billion-sports-stadiums-fewer-seats-higher-prices/
- **$33B, 1970–2020, and the 73% median public share:** the figures are correct, but Fortune got them second-hand.
  - They come from Bradbury, Coates & Humphreys in the *Journal of Economic Surveys* (see item 6).
  - The $33B covers state and local money for major-league venues in the U.S. and Canada.
  - The 73% is the median public share of construction cost among venues that received public money. Fortune called them all "arenas," which is loose.
- **$13B+ proposed in 2024:** verified, but the original source is the Tax Foundation (Hoffer, Johns, Depken, Oct. 3, 2024). It counted 12 cities, including $2.4B each for the Bears and the Rays and $1.7B for the Chiefs and Royals. https://taxfoundation.org/blog/sports-stadium-subsidies-taxpayers/
- **Matheson quote:** the reader's version is a paraphrase. The actual words begin: "No one has ever built a new stadium and provided more affordable tickets…" Use that wording.
- **Other Fortune errors:**
  - Fortune says "NFL ticket prices up 173% after inflation, 2015–2025." That matches no source I found. FinanceBuzz has +81% nominal against 36.5% inflation. Don't repeat the 173% figure.
  - Fortune's "2017 survey, 80% of economists" is the IGM survey, which actually shows 83% on a confidence-weighted basis (see item 5).
- **Usable Fortune details:** Bills' new stadium has 11,500 fewer seats (71,608 down to 60,108) and personal seat licenses up to $50,000.

## 2. Bradbury, "How Much Do Sports Stadiums Cost?" — **Verified**

- **Source:** https://jcbradbury.com/stadium-costs/, first published Aug. 5, 2026, updated Aug. 9, 2026.
- **Headline figure:** $51B in public money committed to new major-league venues since the 1920s, including commitments through 2032, in 2024 dollars. The cumulative chart leaves out MLS.
- **Median public share:**
  - All venues: 58% ($227M of $393M).
  - By league: NFL 68%, MLB 70%, NBA 74%, NHL 64%.
  - By era: about 72% in the 1990s, 47% in the 2010s, 38% in the 2020s.
  - The share is falling, but the median public cost per venue is rising: $605M for 2020s venues (2024 dollars), per his financing primer.
  - The 58% is a different measure from the 73% in item 1. It uses all venues over a different period.
- **Method:**
  - Data before 2010 comes from Judith Grant Long's book (Table 2.1). After 2010, Bradbury collected it from media reports.
  - Counts publicly reported capital costs for buildings, land and infrastructure.
  - Excludes operating and maintenance costs, tax abatements and nearby area infrastructure. The page says unreported in-kind land and infrastructure may be missed.
  - Does not name the inflation index used.
- **Largest subsidies:** Chiefs' new stadium, $1.8B public (opening 2031). Thunder arena, $850M public, a 94% share. SoFi Stadium is the most expensive venue, at $6.655B with $0 public.
- **Downloadable dataset:** not on this page. It points to Appendix A of his book, *This One Will Be Different* (Oxford University Press, 2026). https://academic.oup.com/book/62944
  - His 2023 retrospective with Coates and Humphreys links a public dataset at https://www.openicpsr.org/openicpsr/project/184364/version/V1/view, built mostly from Long (2013), in real 2020 dollars.
  - My fetch tool blocked that page, so I couldn't confirm which files it holds.

## 3. Brookings 2016 and the tax-exempt bond bills — **Verified, with newer figures available**

- **Brookings article:** Gold, Drukker & Gayer, Sept. 8, 2016. https://www.brookings.edu/articles/why-the-federal-government-should-stop-spending-billions-on-private-sports-stadiums/
  - $3.2B federal subsidy and $3.7B total federal revenue loss.
  - Covers 45 stadiums built or renovated since 2000, 36 of them subsidized.
- **Yankees #1:** verified. About $1.7B in tax-exempt bonds produced a $431M subsidy, or $492M total revenue loss. The top-10 list: https://www.brookings.edu/articles/top-10-biggest-federal-subsidies-for-pro-stadiums-hint-the-yankees-are-1/
- **Newer peer-reviewed version:** Drukker, Gayer & Gold, *National Tax Journal* 73(1), March 2020, pp. 157–196. https://ideas.repec.org/a/ntj/journl/v73y2020i1p157-196.html
  - 57 stadiums, 43 financed with tax-exempt bonds.
  - $3.6B subsidy to bond issuers and $4.3B total federal revenue loss. Use this rather than the 2016 figure.
  - Bradbury's page cites $5.4B for 2000–2020, which appears to be an inflation-adjusted version.
- **No Tax Subsidies for Stadiums Act of 2025:**
  - H.R. 2434: sponsored by Rep. Glenn Grothman with Rep. Don Beyer. Introduced March 27, 2025, and referred to House Ways and Means.
  - S. 1192: sponsored by Sen. James Lankford with Sen. Cory Booker. Introduced the same day and referred to Senate Finance.
  - Status at my last check: still in committee with no further action. Re-check congress.gov before publishing.
  - Sources: https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr2434/BILLS-119hr2434ih.htm and https://kiowacountypress.net/content/lawmakers-propose-ending-taxpayer-subsidies-sports-stadiums

## 4. Propheter, foregone property taxes — **Partly verified; per-venue figure is wrong**

- **Source:** Geoffrey Propheter, *Major League Sports and the Property Tax* (Springer, December 2022). https://link.springer.com/book/9783031187896
- **Figures from the book review:** https://www.fieldofschemes.com/2024/02/07/20905/stadiums-and-arenas-are-set-to-collect-18b-in-property-tax-breaks-over-their-lifetimes/
  - 105 venues would have owed $654.3M more in property taxes in 2022.
  - Over current leases, about $18B present value at a 3% discount rate.
  - About $7.5B of that would have gone to K-12 schools (roughly 42%).
- **The $22B figure:** Bradbury cites it as Propheter's estimate of foregone local revenue over the remaining lives of venues active in 2021, in 2024 dollars. So it is roughly the $18B figure restated in later dollars.
- **"~$7.5M per venue per year" is wrong.** $654.3M across 105 venues is about $6.2M per venue per year. The reader appears to have turned the $7.5B schools figure into $7.5M per venue.
- **"~40% to schools":** acceptable. The actual share is about 42%.
- **Bonus:** Propheter estimates the Commanders deal could cost D.C. $6B–$25B in foregone revenue (Stateline, Jan. 12, 2026). https://penncapital-star.com/2026/01/12/repub/sports-stadium-deals-hand-ever-more-taxpayer-money-to-billionaires/

## 5. IGM / Clark Center panel, 2017 — **Verified**

- **Date:** Jan. 31, 2017. https://kentclarkcenter.org/?p=3583
- **Statement:** "Providing state and local subsidies to build stadiums for professional sports teams is likely to cost the relevant taxpayers more than any local economic benefits that are generated." (Too long to quote whole; paraphrase or split it.)
- **Unweighted votes:**
  - Of 42 panelists, 34 voted and 8 did not answer.
  - 5 strongly agree, 19 agree, 5 uncertain, 1 disagree, 0 strongly disagree, 4 no opinion.
  - That is about 71% of voters agreeing.
- **Weighted by confidence:** the St. Louis Fed (Scott Wolla, May 2017) reports 83% agree. https://www.stlouisfed.org/publications/page-one-economics/2017/05/01/the-economics-of-subsidizing-sports-stadiums
- **Avoid:** Chicago Booth Review's "57% agree / 2% disagree." It appears to count only plain "agree" and isn't clearly labeled.

## 6. Bradbury, Coates & Humphreys, *Journal of Economic Surveys* 2023 — **Verified**

- **Citation:** "The impact of professional sports franchises and venues on local economies: A comprehensive survey," *J. Economic Surveys*, 2023, pp. 1389–1431, DOI 10.1111/joes.12533. I couldn't confirm the volume and issue numbers.
- **Coverage:** more than 130 studies over more than 30 years.
- **Finding:** "near-universal consensus" that venues do not produce large positive local effects. Fan spending is a transfer from other local spending, not new money.
- **Source of the $33B and 73% figures** (working paper: SSRN 4022547).
- Summary: https://conversableeconomist.com/2024/01/12/professional-sports-and-the-lack-of-local-economic-payoffs/
- **Companion "policy retrospective"** (Jan. 30, 2023, Kennesaw State/Mercatus manuscript): https://www.kennesaw.edu/coles/centers/markets-economic-opportunity/docs/bradbury-coates-humphreys-01-30-2023.pdf
  - Says $35B since 1970 in 2020 dollars. This is the same data as the $33B, measured differently.
  - Contingent valuation benefits are about 13% of construction cost and 16% of public contributions.
  - Projects about $20B more in public outlays by 2030.

## 7. Cobb County / Truist Park — **Partly verified; the $12–15M range is wrong**

- **Source:** J.C. Bradbury, *A Home Run for Cobb? A Comprehensive Report on the Economic Impact of Truist Park and The Battery Atlanta on Cobb County*, Kennesaw State University, March 2022. Single author. https://www.kennesaw.edu/coles/centers/markets-economic-opportunity/docs/bradbury-cobb-report-march-2022.pdf
- **Figure:** a shortfall of about $15M a year ($14.62M), roughly $50 per household. Total annual costs are about $24.9M (2019). The method is retrospective, using synthetic-control estimates of sales and property effects.
- **No $12M figure appears** in any source I checked.
- **County's rebuttal:** debt payments fell to about $4.26M, and the report missed pandemic effects (Axios, Mar. 10, 2022). https://www.axios.com/local/atlanta/2022/03/10/cobb-taxpayers-braves-stadium

## 8. FinanceBuzz NFL game-day costs — **Figures match the source, but the method is weak**

- **Source:** FinanceBuzz, "NFL Inflation," updated Sept. 1, 2026, via StudyFinds (Sept. 11, 2026). https://studyfinds.com/nfl-game-day-costs-jumped-this-decade/
- **Figures:**
  - Game-day cost per person up 72%, from $114 to $196.
  - Average ticket up 81%, from $86 to $155.
  - Detroit up 336%, from $98 to $429.
- **Why it's unreliable:**
  - 2015 data is Team Marketing Report's average season-ticket price.
  - 2025 data is SeatGeek resale prices, because Team Marketing Report published no 2025 edition. Comparing those two measures overstates the increase.
  - Detroit's jump mostly reflects demand for a winning team on the resale market.
  - The basket is one ticket, one beer, one hot dog and half a parking pass, with no inflation adjustment.
- **Better source:** Team Marketing Report's 2024 NFL Fan Cost Index. https://teammarketing.com/?p=18966
  - Average ticket $136.38, up 12.2% in a year. Fan Cost Index (family of four) $691.41, up 8.9%.
  - Detroit had the biggest ticket increase (+41.2%). Green Bay is highest at $197.11.
  - The 2015 figure of about $86 is consistent with FinanceBuzz's base year, but I didn't find Team Marketing Report's own 2015 page.

## 11. Why it happens — **Supported by sources**

- **Leagues control the supply of teams:** Bradbury notes each league has added only 3–11 teams since 1990. Expansion slots are scarce, so a threat to move is credible.
  - The classic reference is Noll & Zimbalist, *Sports, Jobs, and Taxes* (Brookings, 1997). I confirmed the book exists but didn't read its text.
- **Benefits go to a few, costs spread across many:** the elected-body versus referendum pass rates (96% versus 58%) show it.
  - Groothuis, Johnson & Whitehead, *Eastern Economic Journal* 30(4), 2004 (https://ideas.repec.org/p/apl/wpaper/02-09.html) find civic pride alone doesn't explain public support.
- **"Major league city" value (contingent valuation):** Johnson, Groothuis & Whitehead, *J. Sports Economics* 2(1), 2001, pp. 6–21, a study of the Pittsburgh Penguins. https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/jospec/v2y2001i1p6-21.html
  - Public-good value is "far less than the cost of building a new arena."
  - Across studies, such value averages about 13% of construction cost (2023 retrospective).
- **NFL stadium loans:**
  - G-4 loans were up to $200M per team (the Bills and Titans each got $200M), repaid from premium-seat revenue that would otherwise be shared with other clubs.
  - Sportico reports the G-5 program, approved December 2025, raises this to $250M per team.
  - The league has also waived its $700M debt limit, for example $2.25B for the Rams' SoFi Stadium. https://www.sportico.com/leagues/football/2025/nfl-approves-buffalo-bills-debt-waiver-stadium-1234845545/
- **How owners capture revenue:**
  - Brown, Nagel, McEvoy & Rascher, *Sport Marketing Quarterly* 13(4), 2004 (https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/25741), studied NFL teams moving into new stadiums. Local revenue rose 85%, luxury-box revenue 623%, ticket revenue 54%.
  - The Bills' personal seat licenses run up to $50K (Fortune).
  - Commanders' deal includes free riverfront land and exclusive development rights (Stateline).
- **Franchise value after a new stadium:**
  - The same 2004 study found franchise values rose an average of 35% (a small sample of seven teams, 1990s data).
  - Anecdote: the Vikings' Forbes value rose 22%, from $796M to $975M, after the 2012 stadium deal (CBS Minnesota, Sept. 5, 2012).

## 12. Privately financed venues — **Verified; "private" usually still means some public cost**

- **SoFi Stadium:** about $5–6B, privately built by Kroenke, $0 public per Bradbury. Supported by NFL debt waivers and a $200M league loan, plus personal seat licenses from two teams. The Inglewood council approved it 5–0 in 2015 without a public vote. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SoFi_Stadium
- **Chase Center:** about $1.4B, private, on land the Warriors bought from Salesforce. Critics point to public costs, such as a new Muni subway stop. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chase_Center
- **Intuit Dome:** about $2B, owned by Steve Ballmer.
  - Inglewood sold him city land (price not stated).
  - $100M from Ballmer plus $100M from Intuit under a community benefits agreement, mostly for affordable housing (Fast Company, Sept. 19, 2024). https://www.fastcompany.com/91192646/intuit-dome-inglewood-clippers-ballmer
- **Climate Pledge Arena:** $1.15B, privately financed by Oak View Group. The city owns the building and receives rent plus revenue sharing. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_Pledge_Arena
- **Oracle Park (2000):** $357M.
  - Paid for with a $170M loan and $172M from naming rights, sponsorships and charter seats.
  - Public money: $15M in redevelopment tax-increment financing and about $80M for nearby infrastructure.
  - It came after Bay Area voters repeatedly rejected public ballpark measures.
  - https://sabr.org/bioproj/park/oracle-park-san-francisco/
- **MetLife Stadium:** $1.6B, private. New Jersey spent about $250M on transportation and infrastructure (WNYC, Nov. 29, 2013). https://wnyc.org/story/will-super-bowl-allow-new-jersey-recoup-money-spent-metlife-stadium/
- **What these have in common:** owners with very large markets or very large personal fortunes, voters who had refused to pay, and premium seating, naming rights and real-estate income that covered the cost.

## Best evidence for the writer

- **Scale:** $51B in public commitments for major-league venues through 2032, in 2024 dollars (Bradbury, 2026). For the academic figure, use $33B in 1970–2020 with a 73% median public share (*J. Economic Surveys*, 2023).
- **Consensus:** more than 130 studies over 30 years find no large local economic gains, and on the IGM panel about 83% agreed on a confidence-weighted basis (1 of 34 voters disagreed).
- **Hidden costs:** $4.3B in lost federal revenue from tax-exempt bonds since 2000 (*National Tax Journal*, 2020). About $18B in foregone property taxes, roughly $22B in 2024 dollars, with about $7.5B taken from K-12 schools (Propheter).
- **Record deals:** Kansas up to $1.8B for the Chiefs (about 60% of a $3B stadium), Commanders more than $1B plus land, Titans about $1.26B, Bills $850M.
- **Democracy gap:** elected bodies approve 96% of stadium proposals, but only 58% of referendums pass. Jackson County voted 58% "no" in 2024. Illinois voters oppose public money for the Bears 58% to 32% (Emerson, January 2026).
- **Who captures the gains:** new stadiums raised NFL teams' local revenue 85%, luxury-box revenue 623% and franchise value 35% (Brown et al., 2004).
- **Fans pay twice:** fewer seats and higher prices. The Bills' new stadium has 11,500 fewer seats and personal seat licenses up to $50K. The average NFL ticket was $136.38 in 2024, up 12.2% in one year (Team Marketing Report).
- **Civic pride doesn't close the gap:** it is worth about 13% of construction cost on average.
- **Private financing is possible** (SoFi, Chase Center, Intuit Dome, Climate Pledge, Oracle Park), usually where voters refused or the owner is extremely rich. Even then, some public money goes into infrastructure.
- **Don't use:** Fortune's "173% after inflation," FinanceBuzz's 336% Detroit figure, "$7.5M per venue," the "$12M" Cobb figure, or Matheson's quote as the reader worded it.

**Pages I couldn't read:** The Conversation's original page (blocked; I used the reprint), the OpenICPSR dataset page and jcbradbury.com's home page (blocked by my fetch tool), and the EBSCO abstract for the 2023 survey.
